# Addendum to the v9 fact sheet and points (2026-10-16)

## Mao's words (translated)

- M6. "Embedding the whole corpus is only possible when conditions allow. And how is this different from k-means clustering?"
- Decision: k-means selection is existing practice and is cited as such. The paper's contribution is measuring how much such a sample covers, against a random sample, at a resolution with a content meaning; when it pays; and a pilot that tells this in advance. The method states the conditions for embedding the whole corpus and the fallbacks.

## Facts (verified 2026-10-16)

- P1. Prior uses of one document per k-means group, with the number of groups equal to the budget:
  Chang et al. 2021 [chang2021training] choose training examples for few-shot text generation by clustering the unlabeled data with k-means and taking from each cluster the example closest to its centre.
  Yuan et al. 2020 [yuan2020coldstart] start active learning for text classification by running k-means on embeddings of unlabeled sentences and annotating the sentence nearest each centre.
  Hacohen et al. 2022 [hacohen2022active] cluster the data into as many groups as the budget and label the most typical point of each group.
  Zhang et al. 2023 [zhang2023autocot] group questions with k-means and take one question from each group as an example shown to a large language model.
  Su et al. 2023 [su2023selective] select diverse and representative documents to annotate for a large language model with a graph of nearest neighbours.
  These studies judge the chosen documents by how well a model trained or prompted with them performs. None of them reports how much of the corpus lies within a distance of the same content of a chosen document.
- E1. Conditions for embedding the whole corpus: the whole corpus is in hand; a GPU is available; the data may be processed by an encoder run locally. Measured in this project: 80,634 documents were embedded on one GPU in under 10 minutes with Qwen3-Embedding-8B.
- E2. Fallback when only part of the corpus can be embedded: draw a random pool of m documents, embed the pool, and choose the n documents by k-means within the pool. Tested on nine corpora (Bluesky left out, as before), at both levels, n = 15 and 100, five pools each.
  With a pool of 5,000: the missing mass of the chosen documents in the whole corpus is on average 1.2 percentage points above that of k-means on the whole corpus, at most 5.6 points (Census trump slice, content level, 15 documents: 64% against 59%; a random sample of 15 misses 88%). The pool keeps a median 91% of the gain of k-means over random sampling.
  With a pool of 1,000: on average 3.5 points above, at most 9.8 points (Census trump slice, content level, 100 documents: 56% against 47%; random 66%). The pool keeps a median 69% of the gain. In every case the chosen documents miss less than a random sample of the same size.
  The researcher can compute the missing mass from the pool alone: the percentage of the pool documents that were not chosen and have no chosen document within r. It lies within 1.4 percentage points of the missing mass in the whole corpus with a pool of 5,000 (0.3 on average), and within 2.3 points with a pool of 1,000 (0.6 on average). The chosen documents are left out of the count because each covers itself, the same reason as the factor 1 - n/N.
- E3. Fallback when documents can only be drawn at random and the corpus cannot be embedded (for example, posts drawn from a platform): read the random sample and estimate the missing mass with G (1 - n/N). When N is unknown but far larger than n, 1 - n/N is close to 1 and G alone estimates the missing mass.

## Points (one plain sentence per paragraph; replace or edit the named paragraphs)

A. Abstract, the sentences on choosing (replace "With the whole corpus embedded, a researcher can divide it into as many groups ..." through "... about half as much of the corpus as 15 random documents."):
1. Researchers who label data for models already choose documents this way: they divide the embedded corpus into as many groups of similar documents as they can read and read the document nearest the centre of each group (P1).
2. We measure what such a chosen sample covers: on corpora that repeat themselves it covers far more than the same number of random documents, and at the topic level 15 chosen documents often miss about half as much as 15 random ones (G2, G3 of the v9 sheet).
3. When only part of the corpus can be embedded, choosing within a random pool of 5,000 documents keeps most of the gain (E2).

B. Introduction, paragraph 5 (the one beginning "Annotation and summarization usually draw ..."):
1. Annotation and summarization usually draw the documents they read at random, yet work on labeling data for models chooses them: it divides the embedded corpus with k-means into as many groups as the budget and takes the document nearest each centre (P1).
2. That work judges the chosen documents by how well a model trained on them performs, and does not say how much of the corpus they cover (P1); a survey makes a similar choice when it divides its population into strata.
3. Whether choosing helps depends on how much the corpus repeats itself (keep the existing two sentences on repetitive and diverse corpora).
4. Choosing needs the whole corpus embedded, which is possible only when the corpus is in hand, and otherwise a researcher can choose within a random pool or read a random sample and estimate what it missed (E1 to E3).

C. Introduction, contribution 2 (rewrite):
A measurement of how much a sample chosen by k-means, a practice already used to pick data to label, covers against a random sample at a resolution with a content meaning; it covers far more on corpora that repeat themselves, and the isolation rate of a small random pilot tells in advance how much choosing will save.

D. Method, subsection "Choosing the documents to read" (rewrite the first two paragraphs; keep the greedy paragraph):
1. Choosing needs the whole corpus embedded; state the three conditions and the measured cost (E1).
2. The k-means design, with its prior uses (P1): divide the corpus into n groups, read the document nearest each centre; we take it from that work and ask how much of the corpus it covers.
3. New paragraph after the greedy paragraph: two fallbacks. A random pool of m documents, embedded and chosen within, with the missing mass computed over the pool documents not chosen (E2, method only, no results numbers). And when nothing can be embedded, a random sample read with G (1 - n/N), or G when N is unknown (E3).

E. Results, subsection "Choosing the documents to read": one new paragraph after the paragraph on k-means against greedy:
1. Choosing within a random pool keeps most of the gain: with a pool of 5,000 the chosen sample misses on average 1.2 points more than k-means on the whole corpus, at most 5.6, and with a pool of 1,000 on average 3.5 and at most 9.8; it always misses less than a random sample (E2).
2. The missing mass computed in the pool lies within 1.4 points of the truth with 5,000 and 2.3 with 1,000 (E2).

F. Background, the paragraph beginning "Two other lines of work use distances between embedded documents to choose documents": add one sentence group before "Both need the whole embedded corpus":
Work on labeling data for models chooses one document per k-means group (P1, cite all five), and judges the choice by the performance of the trained model; none measures how much of the corpus the choice covers.

G. Discussion, step three of the five steps (rewrite one sentence): choose the sample with k-means on the embedded corpus, or within a random pool of 5,000 when the whole corpus cannot be embedded; when no embedding is possible, read a random sample and estimate the missing mass with G (1 - n/N).
