\begin{abstract}
If we read 15 documents from a corpus, how much of the corpus have we seen? Researchers who study text read samples because they cannot analyze every document. A survey sample comes with a formula for its size and a margin of error, and a sample of text comes with neither. We measure what a sample has missed as the percentage of the corpus with no document of the same content in the sample. A text encoder turns each document into an embedding, a list of numbers, so that documents with similar meaning have embeddings close together. Two large language models judged pairs of documents to tell which distance between two embeddings means the same specific content and which means the same topic. We estimate the missed percentage from the sample alone with a classical estimator built on the isolation rate, the percentage of sampled documents with no other sampled document of the same content. The main lesson is that a random sample is a poor use of a reading budget on corpora that repeat themselves. With the whole corpus embedded, a researcher can divide it into as many groups of similar documents as the budget allows. The documents nearest the centres of these groups cover far more of such a corpus than the same number of random documents. At the level of topics, 15 documents chosen this way often miss about half as much of the corpus as 15 random documents. On corpora whose documents rarely share specific content, no choice of documents helps at the level of specific content, because a document unlike every other is covered only by reading it. The isolation rate of a random pilot of 100 documents tells a researcher in advance which of the two cases applies. To plan the size of a sample, we show that on most corpora we tested the isolation rate follows a law in which a larger sample and a looser definition of the same content trade against each other in a ratio set by the intrinsic dimension of the corpus, the number of independent directions of meaning along which its documents differ. We solve the law for the number of documents to obtain a sample size formula. In 77 of 83 tests, the sample actually needed fell between two thirds and one and a half times the formula's answer. A pilot of 2{,}000 documents shows in advance whether the formula can be trusted for a given corpus.
\end{abstract}
