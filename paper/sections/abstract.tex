\begin{abstract}
If we read 15 documents from a corpus, how much of the corpus have we seen? A survey comes with a formula for the sample size and a margin of error for its estimates, and a sample of text comes with neither. We measure what a sample has missed as the percentage of the corpus that has no document with the same content in the sample. Two documents count as the same content when their embeddings lie within a distance $r$ that the researcher chooses, and we use judgments by large language models to tie $r$ to content. A classical estimator computes this percentage from the sample itself, by counting how many sampled documents are the only one of their kind in the sample. We show that, on most corpora we tested, a larger sample and a looser distance trade against each other at a fixed rate, set by the intrinsic dimension $d$ of the corpus, which counts the independent directions along which its documents differ. Making the distance twice as strict requires a sample $2^{d}$ times as large. This rate gives a formula for the sample size. We tested it across corpora, coverage targets and distances. In 77 of 83 tests, the sample size actually needed fell within an interval from two thirds to one and a half times the formula's answer, so an answer of 1{,}500 documents meant between 1{,}000 and 2{,}250. A pilot of 2{,}000 documents shows in advance whether the formula holds for a corpus, by fitting it on part of the pilot and checking it on the rest. The sample a researcher needs depends strongly on the corpus. On corpora that repeat a few claims, 15 documents already include a document on the same topic for nearly every document in the corpus. On corpora whose documents rarely share content, 1{,}000 documents still leave most of the corpus without a document on the same specific content.
\end{abstract}
