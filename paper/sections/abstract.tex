\begin{abstract}
A sample drawn from a survey population comes with a margin of error and a formula for the sample size a target precision needs. A sample drawn from a corpus to annotate or to summarize comes with neither. We supply analogues of both for one question: what share of the corpus lies within a chosen cosine distance $r$ of some sampled document, in a fixed embedding space. The share that does not is the missing mass at resolution $r$. Its estimator $G$ needs no labels: it is the share of sampled documents with no other sampled document within $r$ \citep{maurer2022missing}. Under the classical nearest neighbour law, $G$ should depend on sample size $n$ and resolution $r$ only through $n r^{d}$, with $d$ the intrinsic dimension of the embedded corpus. We test this on seventeen pairs of corpus and encoder, including forum, census and political social media text. Where the law holds, a fit on 1{,}000 documents predicts $G$ at seven times that size with an out of sample $R^2$ above 0.92. The sample size the law prescribes for a target isolation rate falls within a factor of 1.5 of the empirical one in 77 of 83 cases. The ecological Chao and Jost extrapolation errs two to nine times more. For a finite corpus of $N$ documents the missing mass is $G\,(1 - n/N)$, to a mean error of 0.012 at sampling fractions up to 0.7. The law fails on corpora whose effective dimension changes with the radius, such as slogans beside discussion, and a fit inside the pilot flags them before the sample is drawn. With $r$ calibrated to mean the same specific content, the law fitted on a pilot predicts, to within four percentage points, the share of a corpus a sample needs to cover half of it: a few hundred posts for most corpora that repeat a few claims, and over 40\% of the corpus for forum questions, stories and Truth Social threads, as if almost no two documents shared content.
\end{abstract}
