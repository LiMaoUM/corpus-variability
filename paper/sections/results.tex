\section{Results}

\subsection{The isolation rate tracks the true coverage}

Before testing the law, we check that $G$ measures what it should. In the synthetic Zipf mixtures every document belongs to a known topic, so we know exactly which percentage of the topics a sample has covered. Over four settings of the mixture, three random seeds and eleven sample sizes from 50 to 5{,}000, the coverage $1 - G$ at a resolution of half the median distance between documents in a pilot follows the true topic coverage closely. The rank correlation is $\rho = 0.968$, and $1 - G$ is off by 2.9 percentage points on average (Appendix~\ref{app:smoke}). Good and Turing's estimator applied to the true topic labels, which $G$ never sees, does only slightly better ($\rho = 0.989$). At smaller resolutions the agreement falls away. At those resolutions two documents of the same topic often lie farther apart than $r$, so $G$ counts a document as unseen content even when its topic is in the sample. The resolution must therefore match the question being asked of the sample, and Section~\ref{sec:rcal} ties it to judgments of content.

\subsection{The isolation rate collapses on $n r^{d}$}
\label{sec:collapse}

Table~\ref{tab:collapse} reports the fit of Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on 17 pairs of corpus and encoder, and Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} shows six of them. On ten pairs the law holds: the three synthetic manifolds, AskDocs and TIFU under both encoders, the random slice of the Census tweets, Truth Social and Bluesky. On these ten, a fit on samples of up to 1{,}000 documents explains 92\% or more of the variance of $\log(-\log G)$ in samples of up to 7{,}000 documents. A fit on samples of up to 300 documents still explains more than 92\%, although it predicts samples 23 times larger than any it saw. In terms of $G$ itself, the law predicts the isolation rate of the largest sample with an average error between 1.5 and 4.2 percentage points on these ten pairs (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). A researcher who has drawn a few hundred documents can thus predict from them alone what percentage of a sample twenty times larger will be isolated, at every resolution at once.

In each panel of Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}, the points of one colour are the values of $G$ at one resolution, for sample sizes from 100 to 7{,}000. For AskDocs and TIFU, the points of all colours lie on one curve once the horizontal axis is $n r^{d}$, so that a larger sample and a looser resolution are interchangeable. For Trump and for the Zipf mixture, each colour traces its own curve, and no single $d$ brings them together.

The second estimate of $d$, from the separate coefficients on $\log n$ and $\log r$, agrees with the TwoNN estimate where the law holds. On the ten passing pairs the two estimates differ by at most 19\%. On the seven failing pairs they differ by 27\% to 43\%, and by 46\% on one of the three GTR samples of Trump, and the regression estimate is always the lower one.

The law also predicts better than the two obvious alternatives. Both alternatives use the same samples of up to 1{,}000 documents to predict $G$ at the largest sample. The first alternative fits a separate curve in $n$ at each resolution, so it needs no $d$. On nine of the ten passing pairs its average error is 1.5 to 3.8 times that of the collapse law, and on the random Census slice it does better (an average error of 2.5 against 4.0 percentage points). The law wins because it pools the information from all resolutions through one $d$, while the separate curves each use one resolution. The second alternative is the extrapolation of \citet{chao2012coverage}, the standard ecological tool, which predicts coverage from the counts of singletons and doubletons. We take a singleton to be a sampled document with no other sampled document within $r$, and a doubleton to be one with exactly one. Run within its recommended range, from 1{,}000 to 2{,}000 documents, its average error on the passing pairs is 2 to 9 times that of the collapse law. Both alternatives beat the law on the Zipf mixture, which fails the pilot test.

\subsection{The sample size formula}
\label{sec:nstar}

To test the formula, we treated each pair of corpus and encoder as a researcher would. We fitted Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on samples of up to 1{,}000 documents, and Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} gave the sample size $n^{*}$ needed to bring the isolation rate down to 30\%, 20\% or 10\% at each resolution. A case is one combination of corpus, target and resolution. We kept every case in which the true sample size lies beyond 1{,}000 documents, so that each case is a genuine prediction, and compared $n^{*}$ with the sample size at which $G$ actually reached the target in repeated draws. The ten passing pairs give 83 such cases, between three and seventeen per pair (Appendix~\ref{app:nstar}).

On the ten passing pairs, the formula comes close in 77 of 83 cases, in the following sense: the sample that actually reached the target held between two thirds of $n^{*}$ and one and a half times $n^{*}$. A formula answer of 1{,}500 documents thus meant an actual need between 1{,}000 and 2{,}250 documents. When $a$ and $b$ are fitted inside one pilot of 2{,}000 documents, as a user would fit them, with $d$ averaged over five pilots, the formula comes close in 76 of 83 cases. On the seven failing pairs it comes close in only 10 of 23 cases, and in 10 of 18 if the Zipf mixture is left out (Appendix~\ref{app:nstar}).

To see how much a single value of $n^{*}$ can be trusted, we drew 50 pilots, refitted $d$, $a$ and $b$ on each, and took the spread of $n^{*}$ across pilots as an interval. These intervals are narrow, with a median width of 19\% of $n^{*}$, and they contain the true sample size in only 21\% of the cases. (This check used its own random draws, in which two of the 83 true sample sizes fell below 1{,}000, so it covers 81 cases.) The error of $n^{*}$ therefore comes mostly from the shape of the law, which is an approximation, and only a little from which pilot was drawn. We report $n^{*}$ with the band observed in the tests instead: the true need lies between two thirds and one and a half times $n^{*}$. The band contains the true sample size in 93\% of the cases on passing corpora. On every passing corpus it contains most of the cases, and the worst corpus is Bluesky, with three of its five cases inside the band.

\subsection{The pilot test}
\label{sec:diag}

The pilot test must work before the sample is drawn, so we compute it inside one pilot of 2{,}000 documents. The ten passing pairs reach a pilot $R^2$ of 0.96 or more, and the seven failing pairs reach at most 0.93 (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). A threshold of 0.95 on the pilot therefore sorts every pair the same way as the full test on the whole corpus. We chose the threshold on these corpora, so it should be confirmed on others. The agreement of the two estimates of $d$ gives the same sorting independently: the two differ by at most 19\% on every passing pair and by more than a quarter on every failing pair.

The law should fail when the effective dimension of a corpus changes with the resolution, and the probes show such a change on Trump (Table~\ref{tab:probe} in Appendix~\ref{app:probe}). Its twenty $k$-means clusters differ widely in local dimension. Clusters with a dimension near 2 hold slogans, short posts repeated in many small variations, and clusters with a dimension near 16 hold discussion. At small resolutions the isolated documents come mainly from one kind of cluster, and at large resolutions mainly from the other, so no single $d$ lines up the curves. Removing the 3.4\% of documents that have a near duplicate leaves the failure in place. The Zipf mixture fails for a related reason: at small resolutions its documents look like noise around 200 topic centres, and at large resolutions they look like 200 points. The probes do not separate the failing Census slices from the passing corpora. Our best guess is that these slices were selected by a keyword that often appears in slogans and calls to action, but we have no direct evidence for it.

\subsection{The finite population correction}
\label{sec:fpc}

The correction matters as soon as a sample is a noticeable part of the corpus. We compared $G$ and $G\,(1 - n/N)$ with the true missing mass $M$ on the eight forum and Census corpora under Qwen3, at sampling fractions from 1\% to 70\% of the corpus and three resolutions, 144 cases in all (Table~\ref{tab:fpc} in Appendix~\ref{app:fpc}). Without the correction, $G$ is off by 11 percentage points on average and by as much as 49 points. With it, the estimate is off by 1.2 points on average and by at most 11 points. When half of a corpus is sampled, the uncorrected $G$ reports about twice the unseen percentage that is actually there (0.40 against 0.20 in the average over corpora). On the three GTR forum corpora and the Zipf mixture the correction lowers the average error from 6.8 to 1.5 points. The correction holds on the corpora that fail the collapse law, because it only uses the fact that sampled documents are covered.

\subsection{Calibrated resolutions}
\label{sec:rcal}

On every corpus, the percentage of pairs that the judge calls the same content falls steadily as the distance grows. Where it crosses one half, however, depends on the corpus (Table~\ref{tab:rcal}). Under Qwen3.8, $r_{\mathrm{same}}$ lies at 0.05 or below on TIFU and reaches 0.28 on the Census tweets that contain the word ``illegal''. On Bluesky fewer than half of even the closest pairs are judged the same content, so no distance qualifies. Long personal stories differ in their details even when their embeddings are close, while short posts that repeat a slogan remain the same content at a larger distance. The distance at which two documents share a topic varies just as much, from 0.33 on TIFU to 0.60 on AskDocs. On the Census tweets that contain the word ``citizens'', every judged pair closer than 0.4 shares at least a topic, so the whole slice is in effect one topic. A cosine distance therefore has no fixed meaning across corpora, and a user who wants $r$ to mean ``the same claim'' has to judge a few hundred pairs from the corpus at hand.

A second model, Gemma-4-31B-it, judged the same pairs with the same prompt. The two models give the same label to 71\% of pairs (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.55$, and $\kappa = 0.68$ for the same content against the other two labels). Gemma calls more pairs the same content than Qwen3.8 does, 39\% against 28\%, so its crossing lies farther out on every corpus. Nine corpora have a crossing under both judges, since Bluesky has none. On six of these nine the 90\% intervals of the two crossings do not overlap. The choice of judge therefore moves $r_{\mathrm{same}}$ more than the sampling of pairs does. The judge is therefore part of the definition of $r$, and a user should report it together with the encoder.

The table shows two groups of corpora. On TIFU, AskDocs, Truth Social and the random Census slice, a sample covers almost nothing at the level of specific content. Fifteen documents leave more than 99\% of the corpus with nothing like it in the sample, and 1{,}000 documents still leave between 84\% and 96\%. The documents of these corpora rarely share specific content, so each sampled document stands only for itself. At the level of topic the same corpora differ sharply. Fifteen questions from AskDocs leave 58\% of the corpus without a question on the same topic, and 100 questions leave 15\%. Fifteen stories from TIFU leave 98.5\% without a story on the same topic, and even 1{,}000 stories leave more than half, because in the judge's view two personal stories rarely share a topic.

The second group, the Trump forum and the Census slices selected by a keyword, behaves differently. Fifteen posts already show the reader almost every topic, leaving between 1.8\% and 11\% of the corpus without a post on the same topic. At the level of specific content, 15 posts leave between 62\% and 90\% of the corpus unseen. A sample of 1{,}000 posts still leaves between 16\% and 42\%. These corpora repeat a limited number of claims, so a sample of moderate size contains a large part of what is said.

The answer to the question of the introduction is therefore conditional. Fifteen documents give a fair picture of the topics of a corpus that repeats a few claims, a poor picture of the topics of a corpus of personal stories, and a poor picture of the specific content of any corpus we studied. A reader does not need the whole corpus to learn which case applies. Once $r$ is set, the sample itself shows which case applies. Take a random sample of 15 of the 17{,}202 Census tweets that contain the word ``illegal''. In such a sample, 64\% of the tweets are the only one of their kind in the sample, so the estimate says that about 64\% of the corpus has nothing with the same content in the sample. The true value, computed against the whole corpus, is 62\%. Across corpora, the estimate $G\,(1 - n/N)$ computed from the 15 sampled documents alone, averaged over the same 20 samples, lies within 6 percentage points of the true percentage on every corpus and at both levels, and within 1.6 points on average. With 100 documents it lies within 2 points. The test is easy on the corpora where nearly everything is unseen, since there $G$ is almost always 1. It is informative on the repetitive corpora, where $G$ falls well below 1 and still follows the truth.

\subsection{The sample that covers half of a corpus}
\label{sec:feasible}

A researcher who wants a sample to stand for most of a corpus at the level of specific content can ask how large the sample must be. We take a modest target, that half of the corpus should have a document with the same content in the sample. This target concerns the corpus, through the corrected missing mass $G\,(1 - n/N)$, while the formula of Section~\ref{sec:nstar} targets the isolation rate $G$ of the sample itself. Table~\ref{tab:rcal} gives the random sample that reaches this target on each corpus and under each judge. We measured it on the whole corpus, as the sample size at which $G(n, r_{\mathrm{same}})\,(1 - n/N)$ falls to one half, with three random samples at each size up to $0.7N$. A useful reference point is a corpus in which no two documents share content. In such a corpus the sample covers only itself, so reaching the target takes exactly half of the corpus.

The corpora fall into the same two groups as in Table~\ref{tab:reader}. On the Trump forum and the Census slices with the keywords citizens, trump and illegal, which repeat a few claims, at most 604 documents reach the target under either judge. On AskDocs, TIFU and Truth Social the sample must hold between 41\% and 50\% of the corpus, close to the 50\% of a corpus with no shared content at all. For these corpora, a sample covers what it contains and little more, and an analysis that needs every specific claim in the corpus has to read the corpus. At the calibrated distance, a pilot of only 100 documents tells the two groups apart. In a pilot of 100, the isolation rate $G$ is 1.00 on AskDocs, TIFU and Truth Social, so no sampled document has a match in the pilot. On the repetitive corpora it is at most 0.90.

Two corpora depend on the judge. The Census slice with the keyword covid needs 19\% of the corpus under Qwen3.8 and 1.6\% under Gemma. The random Census slice, drawn without any keyword, needs 44\% under Qwen3.8 and 7\% under Gemma. Under the stricter judge these corpora look like collections of distinct documents, and under the looser judge they look repetitive.

The collapse law fitted on a pilot can predict this sample size before the corpus is embedded. We fitted $d$, $a$ and $b$ on one pilot of 2{,}000 documents, computed $G(n, r_{\mathrm{same}})$ for every $n$ from Equation~\ref{eq:collapse}, applied the correction $1 - n/N$, and found where the result falls to one half. The prediction puts every corpus in the same group as the measurement. How close it comes depends on where the target is reached (Appendix~\ref{app:halfcover}). When the target is reached below 100 documents, the prediction is below 100 too. When it is reached at the 50\% limit, as on TIFU and AskDocs, the prediction is right almost by necessity, and these cases test little. When it is reached between 100 and 1{,}000 documents, the predicted sample is at most a third larger or a quarter smaller than the measured one. On the Census trump slice, for example, the law predicts 472 documents and the measurement gives 604. In the four cases that lie beyond the pilot's range, the prediction is 8\% too high on the Census covid slice. On Truth Social and the random Census slice the measured percentage is 41\% to 44\%. There the prediction is about 4 percentage points too high. The law thus places these corpora correctly near the limit, and it places them a little too close, so it advises a slightly larger sample than needed.

The distance $r_{\mathrm{same}}$ can also be found inside the pilot, so that the whole procedure runs on 2{,}000 documents. On five corpora, Gemma judged up to 400 pairs drawn within the first pilot, and $d$, $a$ and $b$ came from the same pilot (Appendix~\ref{app:pilotcal}). On the random Census slice, Truth Social, the Trump forum and AskDocs, the 90\% interval of the pilot's crossing contains the crossing found with pairs from the whole corpus, and the percentage predicted from the pilot alone is within 2.5 percentage points of the measured percentage. In TIFU, the pilot of 2{,}000 stories contains no pair closer than 0.15, so it cannot locate a crossing that lies below that distance. It can only say that the crossing, if there is one, lies below 0.15, and at that bound the law predicts that half of the corpus is needed, as measured. TIFU and AskDocs sit at the 50\% limit, where the answer hardly depends on $r$, so they test the calibration inside the pilot only weakly. The random Census slice, Truth Social and the Trump forum carry the test.
