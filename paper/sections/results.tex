\section{Results}

\subsection{The estimator tracks true topic coverage}

The Zipf mixtures give every document a topic, so the true topic mass covered by a sample is known. Over four settings, three seeds and eleven sample sizes from 50 to 5{,}000, coverage $1 - G$ at a radius of half the median pairwise distance of a pilot tracks the true coverage with Spearman $\rho = 0.968$ and a mean absolute error of 0.029 (Appendix~\ref{app:smoke}). Good-Turing on the true labels, which $G$ does not see, reaches $\rho = 0.989$. At smaller radii the agreement falls away. The radius matters, and the collapse law gives the dependence on it.

\subsection{The isolation rate collapses on $n r^{d}$}
\label{sec:collapse}

Table~\ref{tab:collapse} reports the fit of Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} for 17 pairs of corpus and encoder. Ten pass the diagnostic: the three manifolds, AskDocs and TIFU under both encoders, the random census slice, Truth Social and Bluesky. On these ten, a fit on cells with $n \le 1{,}000$ explains 92\% or more of the variance of $\log(-\log G)$ on cells with $n$ up to 7{,}000. Fitting on $n \le 300$ instead, a 23-fold extrapolation, keeps it above 0.92. Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} shows the surfaces against $n r^{d}$.

The fit with $\log n$ and $\log r$ as separate regressors gives a second estimate of $d$ that does not use TwoNN. On the ten passing pairs the two estimates differ by at most 19\%. On the seven failing pairs they differ by 27\% to 43\%, with the regression estimate always lower.

Two baselines predict $G$ at the largest $n$ from the cells with $n \le 1{,}000$. A separate curve in $n$ at each radius, which needs no $d$, errs 1.5 to 3.8 times more than the collapse law on nine of the ten passing pairs, and less on the random census slice (0.025 against 0.040). The Chao and Jost extrapolation from singletons and doubletons \citep{chao2012coverage}, run within its recommended range of a doubling from $n = 1{,}000$ to 2{,}000, errs 2 to 9 times more than the collapse law on the passing pairs. Both baselines beat the collapse law on the Zipf mixture, which fails the diagnostic.

\subsection{The sample size formula}

For every pair, Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} was fitted on cells with $n \le 1{,}000$, and Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} gave $n^{*}$ for targets $\varepsilon \in \{0.3, 0.2, 0.1\}$ at every radius where the mean surface crosses $\varepsilon$ beyond $n = 1{,}000$. The prediction was compared with the crossing interpolated on the log scale between grid points. On the ten passing pairs, 77 of 83 predictions fall within a factor of 1.5 of the empirical sample size. On the seven failing pairs, 10 of 23 do (Appendix~\ref{app:nstar}).

The error of $n^{*}$ comes from the shape of the law more than from the pilot. Resampling the pilot 50 times and refitting $d$, $a$ and $b$ each time gives intervals with a median width of 19\% of $n^{*}$, and these cover the empirical crossing in only 21\% of cases. We therefore report $n^{*}$ with the empirical band of a factor of 1.5, which held in 93\% of cases on passing corpora.

A random sample can be compared with a sample chosen for coverage. Greedy farthest point selection, the core-set construction of \citet{sener2018coreset}, minimises the largest distance from any document to the chosen set. To bring the missing mass at a radius below a target, a random sample needs 0.6 to 1.0 times as many documents as greedy selection on the forum and census corpora, and 0.12 times as many on Truth Social and Bluesky. Greedy selection spends its picks on outlying documents, which are few, while the missing mass weighs documents equally.

\subsection{The diagnostic}

The diagnostic must work before the sample is drawn, so we compute it inside one pilot of 2{,}000 documents: fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of size up to 750, and score $R^2$ on subsamples of size 1{,}000 to 2{,}000. The ten passing pairs reach a pilot $R^2$ of 0.96 or more, and the seven failing pairs reach at most 0.93 (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). A pilot threshold of 0.95 therefore reproduces the full diagnostic on every pair. The threshold was chosen on these corpora, and the agreement of the two dimension estimates gives a second check that needs no threshold from them: on every failing pair the two differ by more than a quarter.

Section~3.2 says when the law should fail: when the effective dimension changes with the radius. On Trump the probes show such a change (Table~\ref{tab:probe}). Its twenty $k$-means clusters differ most in local dimension: clusters of dimension near 2 hold slogans, and clusters near 16 hold discussion. At small radii the slogan clusters, where documents sit on a nearly flat set, dominate the count of isolated documents, and at large radii the discussion clusters do. Removing the 3.4\% of documents with a near duplicate leaves the failure in place. The Zipf mixture changes dimension with radius for another reason: at small radii its documents look like noise around their centres, and at large radii they look like 200 points. The probes do not separate the failing census slices from the passing corpora, and we do not have a mechanism for them beyond their selection on a keyword that recurs in slogans and calls to action.

\subsection{The finite population correction}
\label{sec:fpc}

Table~\ref{tab:fpc} compares $G$ and $G\,(1 - n/N)$ with the true missing mass $M$ of the finite corpus on the eight forum and census corpora under Qwen3, at sampling fractions from 0.01 to 0.7 and three radii, 144 cells. Uncorrected, $G$ has a mean absolute error of 0.114 and a maximum of 0.49. Corrected, the mean error is 0.012 and the maximum 0.11, and at $n/N = 0.5$ the error is 0.004. On the three GTR forum corpora and the Zipf mixture the correction cuts the error from 0.068 to 0.015. The correction holds on the corpora that fail the collapse.

\subsection{Choosing the resolution}
\label{sec:rcal}

The resolution $r$ is a cosine distance, and a user needs to know what content it groups. For each of the ten real corpora under Qwen3, we drew about 40 document pairs in each of ten distance bins from 0 to 1, 3{,}913 pairs in all, and asked Qwen3.8-27B whether the two documents are about the same specific content (the same claim, question, event or story), the same general topic, or unrelated (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The share judged the same content falls with distance in every corpus. Where it crosses one half depends on the corpus, from $r = 0.05$ on TIFU to 0.28 on a census slice, and on Bluesky it stays below one half even for the nearest pairs (Table~\ref{tab:rcal}). Long first person stories differ in their details even when they are close in embedding space, while short posts that repeat a slogan are the same content at a larger distance. A cosine distance therefore does not carry one meaning across corpora, and a user who wants $r$ to mean ``the same claim'' should judge a few hundred pilot pairs binned by distance and take the distance where the share of same content crosses the chosen level.

A second model, Gemma-4-31B-it, judged the same pairs with the same prompt. The two agree on 71\% of pairs (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.55$; $\kappa = 0.68$ for same content against the other two labels). Gemma calls more pairs the same content, 39\% against 28\%, so its crossing lies farther out in every corpus, by 0.02 to 0.16, and a user should report the judge along with the encoder.

\subsection{What a target costs}
\label{sec:feasible}

Table~\ref{tab:rcal} gives, under each judge, the sample size at which half of the corpus lies within the same content distance of some sampled document. On the Trump forum corpus and three census slices, whose posts repeat a few claims, between eight and about six hundred documents suffice under either judge, and where the grid reaches the target the empirical sample size is within a factor of 1.6 of the prediction. On AskDocs, TIFU and Truth Social the required sample exceeds the corpus by orders of magnitude under either judge, and $G$ at the largest sample stays above 0.9: almost no document has another about the same specific content, so an analysis that needs every claim must read every document. The census random slice, drawn from the census export with no keyword filter, falls on either side depending on the judge, since its two crossings differ by a factor of 2.3 and $n^{*}$ grows as $r^{-12.9}$. The formula says which situation a corpus is in before any annotation is bought.
