\section{Background}

\subsection{How much a sample has missed: the discrete case}

The question of how much a sample has missed was first answered for samples of categories, such as words in a text or species in a forest. A sample of $n$ draws covers the categories it contains, and the missing mass is the total share of the population that belongs to categories the sample never saw. Let $f_k$ be the number of categories that appear exactly $k$ times in the sample. \citet{good1953} showed that $f_1/n$, the share of draws whose category appears only once, estimates the missing mass with little bias. The intuition is that a category seen once is the kind of category that could easily have been missed, so the number of such categories tells how much was missed. \citet{gale1995} turned the estimator into a standard tool for estimating the frequency of rare words, and \citet{good1956} extended it to predict how much a larger sample would cover.

Ecologists built a whole sampling theory on the same idea, because a field survey of species faces our problem exactly: no survey finds every species, and the surveyor must decide when to stop. \citet{chao1984} bounded the number of species a survey has not found using $f_1$ and $f_2$, the counts of species seen once and twice. \citet{chao2012coverage} then wrote the coverage a survey would reach after $m$ more draws as a formula in $n$, $f_1$ and $f_2$, so that a survey can be planned to stop at a target coverage. Ecologists now treat the curve of coverage against sample size as one object, extended backward to smaller samples and forward to larger ones \citep{colwell2012, chao2014hill}. The same ideas reach well beyond ecology. \citet{efron1976} used them to estimate how many words Shakespeare knew but never used, and \citet{orlitsky2016unseen} showed how far ahead the number of unseen categories can be predicted, up to about $n \log n$ draws for a sample of $n$.

These estimators have been applied to text and to machine learning a few times. \citet{hajic2025} applied the Chao bound to a catalogue of chant to estimate how far the catalogue is from complete. \citet{tran2017} fitted species models to the answers to an open ended survey question to predict when new kinds of answer stop appearing. \citet{chung2018unknown} counted the examples a training set is missing with the same estimator, treating each unique example as a species, and \citet{pal2026blindspot} estimated how much of the situations a deployed classifier meets it has never seen in training. In all four studies an item belongs to a discrete category and two items either match or do not. Documents rarely fall into such categories. Two posts can make the same point in different words, and two posts with many words in common can make opposite points.

\subsection{How much a sample has missed: the case of distances}

\citet{maurer2022missing} removed the need for categories. He works in any space with a distance $\delta(\cdot, \cdot)$ between items. For a sample $X_1, \dots, X_n$ and a distance $r$, the missing mass is the probability that a new item lies farther than $r$ from every item in the sample. Maurer calls it the conditional missing mass, because it depends on the sample actually drawn. His estimator is
\begin{equation}
G(X, r) = \frac{1}{n}\,\big|\{k : \delta(X_k, X_i) > r \text{ for all } i \neq k\}\big| ,
\label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
the share of sampled items that have no other sampled item within $r$. When items are categories, so that two items are at distance 0 if they share a category and at distance 1 otherwise, $G$ with $r < 1$ is exactly Good and Turing's $f_1/n$. Maurer proved that $G$ overstates the conditional missing mass by at most $1/n$ on average, and that the variance of the difference between the two is at most $3/n$ (his Theorem 2.1). His paper is theory. It sketches anomaly detection and data compression as uses, and it contains no data, no embeddings and no statement about how $G$ changes as the sample grows.

That change is described by a classical result about nearest neighbours. Suppose items are drawn from a density $f$ on a set of dimension $d$. Then the probability that an item at $x$ has no other item within $r$ among $n$ draws is close to $\exp(-n f(x)\, v_d\, r^{d})$, where $v_d$ is the volume of a ball of radius 1 in $d$ dimensions \citep{evans2002, penrose2003rgg}. The same result is the basis of the correlation dimension of \citet{grassberger1983} and of the estimators of intrinsic dimension proposed by \citet{levina2005} and \citet{facco2017twonn}, which work backward from nearest neighbour distances to $d$. We use it forward, to predict how $G$ falls as $n$ grows. The ecological work closest to ours is the attribute diversity of \citet{chao2019attribute}. There, two species whose traits lie closer than a threshold $\tau$ count as partly the same species, and diversity is reported as a function of $\tau$. We treat $r$ the same way, as a resolution the user chooses for the question at hand.

\subsection{Measures of diversity in embedding space}

Several measures describe how spread out a set of embedded documents is, and none of them says how many documents a sample needs. The Vendi score \citep{friedman2023vendi} is an effective number of distinct items in a sample, computed from the eigenvalues of the matrix of cosine similarities among the items, and \citet{pasarkar2024cousins} extend it to a family of related measures. The precision and recall of \citet{kynkaanniemi2019} count how many generated items fall inside the neighbourhoods of a set of reference items. \citet{naeem2020} made these counts less sensitive to how spread out the reference set itself is, and MAUVE compares two sets of texts through the distributions of their embeddings \citep{pillutla2021mauve}. Each of these measures describes the sample in hand or compares two samples. None predicts what a larger sample would cover, and none has a parameter that plays the role of the population variance in a survey.

Two other lines of work use distances between embedded documents to choose documents. Core set selection picks the documents that make the largest distance from any document to the chosen set as small as possible \citep{sener2018coreset}, and semantic deduplication removes documents that lie within a small distance of another document \citep{abbas2023semdedup, lee2022dedup}. Both need the whole embedded corpus before they start, and neither says how many documents a target requires. The intrinsic dimension of learned representations has also been measured, for images \citep{pope2021intrinsic}, for the layers of deep networks \citep{ansuini2019}, and for text, where it separates human writing from text generated by a model \citep{tulchinskii2023}. We use the intrinsic dimension for a different purpose, as the property of a corpus that sets the sample size.

\subsection{When to stop reading in practice}

Where a sample size for text is set on purpose at all, it is set by a stopping rule. Qualitative researchers stop interviewing at saturation, when new interviews add no new codes. \citet{guest2006} found that saturation came after about twelve interviews in their study, and a review of 23 empirical tests of saturation by \citet{hennink2022} places it between 9 and 17 interviews. \citet{depaoli2024} carried the rule to coding done by a large language model, tracking how fast the count of unique codes grows as more text is coded. In topic discovery with large language models, TopicGPT reports that the number of topics it finds levels off after about 600 documents, and it stops when 200 further documents add no new topic \citep{pham2024topicgpt}. \citet{maeda2025} fitted a topic model with eight topics to 522 open ended survey answers and found that a sample of 100 answers recovers more than 60\% of the topics found in all 522, and a sample of 250 about 70\%. Each of these studies observes one coverage curve and stops on it. None can say, before reading, where the curve will flatten for a new corpus. The present paper gives the curve a parameter, the intrinsic dimension $d$, and an estimator that a researcher can compute from the sample.
