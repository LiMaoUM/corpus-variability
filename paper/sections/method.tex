\section{Method}

\subsection{The missing mass of a corpus sample}

A corpus is a finite set of $N$ documents $x_1, \dots, x_N$. We embed each document with a fixed encoder, scale each embedding to length 1, and measure the distance between two documents by the cosine distance of their embeddings. Two documents with nearly the same meaning are at a distance near 0, and two unrelated documents are at a distance near 1. A sample $S$ is a set of $n$ documents drawn at random without replacement.

The user chooses a resolution $r$. Two documents within $r$ of each other count as the same content for the purpose at hand. A small $r$ asks for close matches, such as two posts that make the same claim, and a large $r$ accepts loose matches, such as two posts on the same topic. Embeddings from current encoders crowd into a narrow region of the space \citep{ethayarajh2019}, so the same number, say $r = 0.2$, means different things for different encoders and corpora. Section~\ref{sec:rcal} therefore ties $r$ to judgments of content.

The quantity we estimate is the missing mass of the sample at resolution $r$,
\begin{equation}
M(S, r) = \frac{1}{N}\,\big|\{j : \delta(x_j, x_i) > r \text{ for all } i \in S\}\big| ,
\end{equation}
the proportion of corpus documents that have no sampled document within $r$. In words, $M$ is the percentage of the corpus that has nothing like it in the sample. If $M = 0.6$, then 60\% of the corpus consists of documents for which the sample holds no document with the same content, and a summary or an annotation built from the sample can say nothing specific about them. Coverage is $1 - M$, the percentage of the corpus that does have something like it in the sample.

Computing $M$ requires the whole embedded corpus, because it compares every corpus document with the sample. The estimator $G$ of Equation~\ref{eq:G} needs only the sample. For each sampled document it finds the nearest other sampled document, and it counts the sampled documents whose nearest sampled neighbour lies farther than $r$. We write $G(n, r)$ for its value on a sample of $n$ documents. Our target is the finite corpus in hand. A researcher samples because the corpus is too large to annotate or to place in a model's context, and in our experiments the whole corpus is available, so we can check every estimate against the true $M$.

\subsection{The collapse law}

The isolation rate $G$ is the percentage of sampled documents that are isolated from the rest of the sample. To plan a sample, we need to know how $G$ falls as $n$ grows, and how that depends on $r$.

Suppose the embedded documents lie close to a set of intrinsic dimension $d$. The intrinsic dimension counts the independent directions along which documents actually vary, which is far smaller than the 768 or 4{,}096 coordinates of an embedding. Around a document $x$, the proportion of the corpus that lies within Euclidean distance $\rho$ is then about $c(x)\, \rho^{d}$, where $c(x)$ is larger where documents are densely packed. Our resolution is a cosine distance. Between embeddings of length 1, the cosine distance is half the squared Euclidean distance, so a cosine distance $r$ corresponds to the Euclidean distance $\sqrt{2r}$. The proportion of the corpus within cosine distance $r$ of $x$ is therefore about $c(x)\, r^{d/2}$, where the constant $2^{d/2}$ is absorbed into $c(x)$. The chance that none of $n$ sampled documents falls within $r$ of $x$ is about $\exp(-c(x)\, n\, r^{d/2})$. Averaging over documents gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\, G(n, r) \approx \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\exp(-c(x)\, n\, r^{d/2})\big] = \mathcal{L}_{c}(n\, r^{d/2}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{c}$ is the Laplace transform of the distribution of $c$ over documents. The argument of $\mathcal{L}_{c}$ is $n r^{d/2}$. Whatever the density looks like, $G$ depends on the sample size and the resolution only through this product, as long as one $d$ holds across the resolutions of interest. We call this the collapse law, because curves of $G$ against $n$ at different resolutions collapse onto one curve when they are plotted against $n r^{d/2}$. On the synthetic manifolds, where we use Euclidean distance, the product is $n r^{d}$.

The product tells a researcher how sample size and resolution trade against each other. Doubling the sample has the same effect on $G$ as making the cosine distance $2^{2/d}$ times as large, which for $d = 26$ is only 5\% larger. In the other direction, halving $r$ requires $2^{d/2}$ times as many documents, about 8{,}000 times for $d = 26$. Corpora with a high intrinsic dimension are therefore expensive to cover at a fine resolution, in the same way that populations with a large variance are expensive to survey precisely.

The shape of the curve depends on how much the density varies across the corpus. If every document sat in a region of the same density, $G$ would fall as a pure exponential in $n r^{d/2}$. Real corpora mix dense regions, where many documents say similar things, with sparse regions, where documents are unusual, and the curve then falls more slowly. We describe the shape by a stretched exponential, $G = \exp(-(\kappa\, n\, r^{d/2})^{b})$ with $0 < b \le 1$, where a smaller $b$ means a more uneven density. Taking logarithms twice turns this into a straight line,
\begin{equation}
\log\big(-\log G(n, r)\big) = a + b \log\big(n\,r^{d/2}\big),
\label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
which we fit by least squares, with $d$ estimated beforehand.

We estimate $d$ with the TwoNN estimator of \citet{facco2017twonn}, which uses, for every document, the ratio of the distance to its second nearest neighbour to the distance to its first. We apply it with Euclidean distance between the unit embeddings, which orders neighbours exactly as cosine distance does, so that $d$ is the dimension of the set itself. We average it over five pilot samples of 2{,}000 documents. To fit $a$ and $b$, we draw five independent samples at each of eleven sample sizes from 100 to 7{,}000 (no more than $0.7N$), and compute $G$ at twelve resolutions spaced evenly on a log scale between the 5th and the 99th percentile of the nearest neighbour distances in a pilot. Only cells with $0.02 < G < 0.98$ enter the fit, because the double logarithm is unstable near 0 and 1.

As a check on $d$ that does not use TwoNN, we also fit $\log(-\log G)$ on $\log n$ and $\log r$ as two separate regressors. If the collapse law holds, the coefficient on $\log r$ equals $b\,d/2$ and the coefficient on $\log n$ equals $b$, so twice their ratio is a second estimate of $d$. When the two estimates of $d$ disagree, no single dimension describes the corpus across the resolutions used.

\subsection{The sample size formula}

Solving Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} for $n$ gives the sample size at which the isolation rate at resolution $r$ falls to a target $\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
n^{*}(\varepsilon, r) = \left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{e^{a}}\right)^{1/b} r^{-d/2} .
\label{eq:nstar}
\end{equation}
For example, $\varepsilon = 0.2$ asks for a sample in which at most 20\% of the documents are isolated, which, by the argument of Good and Turing, is a sample that leaves about 20\% of the corpus unseen. The corpus enters the formula through three numbers. The intrinsic dimension $d$ sets how fast the required sample grows as the resolution becomes finer. The constants $a$ and $b$ set the level and the shape. All three can be estimated from a pilot. Section~\ref{sec:nstar} reports the formula with $a$ and $b$ fitted inside a pilot of 2{,}000 documents, as a user would fit them, and with $a$ and $b$ fitted on draws from the whole corpus.

\subsection{The finite population correction}

$G$ estimates the missing mass of the process that generated the corpus, as if new documents kept arriving. When the target is the corpus itself, two facts change the picture. The $n$ sampled documents are part of the corpus, and each of them has something like it in the sample, namely itself. Each of the other $N - n$ documents is isolated from the sample with a probability close to $G$. The missing mass of the corpus is therefore
\begin{equation}
\hat M(n, r) = G(n, r)\,\big(1 - n/N\big).
\label{eq:fpc}
\end{equation}
If a researcher samples half of a corpus and $G$ is 0.8, then 80\% of the unread half is unseen, which is 40\% of the corpus. The factor $1 - n/N$ is the one survey sampling calls the finite population correction \citep{groves2009survey}. In a survey it shrinks the variance of an estimated mean. Here it shrinks an expected proportion, for the simple reason that the documents in the sample are covered by definition.

\subsection{A test for corpora with more than one scale}

The collapse law assumes that one intrinsic dimension holds across the resolutions of interest, and a researcher needs to know whether that holds before drawing the sample. We therefore test the law inside a pilot of 2{,}000 documents. We fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of the pilot with up to 750 documents, and we compute how much of the variance of $\log(-\log G)$ the fit explains on subsamples of 1{,}000 to 2{,}000 documents, which it did not see. A corpus passes when this pilot $R^2$ is at least 0.95. The two estimates of $d$ give a second test, since they should agree when the law holds.

To check whether the pilot test gives the right answer, we also fit the law on the whole corpus with samples of up to 1{,}000 documents and evaluate it on samples of up to 7{,}000. We call a corpus collapsing when this full out of sample $R^2$ is at least 0.9. When the law fails, two probes help find the reason. The first is the percentage of documents that have a near duplicate, a nearest neighbour within cosine distance 0.02. The second is the spread of the intrinsic dimension across twenty clusters of the corpus found by $k$-means, which shows whether different parts of the corpus vary along different numbers of directions.

\subsection{Calibrating the resolution with judgments of content}

A cosine distance means little to a reader until it is tied to content. For each real corpus, we drew about 40 pairs of documents in each of ten bins of distance between 0 and 1, about 400 pairs per corpus. Near pairs come from the nearest neighbours of random documents, and far pairs come from random pairs. A large language model read each pair and judged whether the two documents are about the same specific content (the same claim, question, event or story), about the same general topic, or unrelated (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The percentage of pairs judged the same content falls as the distance grows. We call $r_{\mathrm{same}}$ the distance at which this percentage crosses one half, interpolated between the centres of neighbouring bins, and $r_{\mathrm{topic}}$ the distance at which the percentage judged the same content or the same topic crosses one half. We obtain a 90\% interval for each crossing by resampling the judged pairs within their bins 2{,}000 times. Two large language models judged every pair, Qwen3.8-27B and Gemma-4-31B-it, so that we can see how much the meaning of $r$ depends on the judge. We call them Qwen3.8 and Gemma in what follows. The encoder Qwen3-Embedding-8B, which we call Qwen3, is a different model from the same family.

\subsection{Choosing the documents to read}
\label{sec:design}

A text encoder embeds a whole corpus without any human reading and without any call to a large language model. A researcher who has embedded the corpus can therefore choose which $n$ documents to read. We compare random samples of $n$ documents with two designs that choose the $n$ documents from the embedded corpus.

In the first design, the researcher divides the corpus into $n$ groups of similar documents and reads the document nearest the center of each group. $k$-means \citep{lloyd1982} forms the groups by assigning each document to the nearest of $n$ centers and moving each center to the average embedding of its documents, until the groups stop changing. For a sample of 15 documents, $k$-means divides the corpus into 15 groups, and the researcher reads one document from each group. Any researcher can run this design, because $k$-means is a standard tool for grouping documents by their embeddings.

The second design, greedy maximum coverage, picks the documents one at a time. Each pick is the document that covers the most corpus documents not yet covered, that is, the most documents with no chosen document within $r$. With 15 documents, the first pick is the document with the most other documents within $r$, and each later pick adds the most documents that the earlier picks left uncovered. A set chosen this way covers at least 63\% of what the best possible set of $n$ documents covers \citep{nemhauser1978}. We use the greedy design as a reference for how much a careful choice of $n$ documents can cover. Because the distance from every corpus document to its nearest chosen document is known, we compute the missing mass of a chosen sample exactly, without an estimator.

\subsection{The resolution a sample reaches}
\label{sec:resolution}

Every random sample covers 80\% of the corpus at some resolution, because a looser resolution $r$ counts more documents as the same content. We call this resolution $r_{80}(n)$, the distance between embeddings within which 80\% of the corpus has a document from a random sample of $n$ documents. We take 80\% as an example of a high level of coverage. We estimate $r_{80}(n)$ from the sample alone as the resolution at which $G\,(1 - n/N)$ equals 20\%, because $G\,(1 - n/N)$ estimates the missing mass. We interpret $r_{80}(n)$ in content through the pairs of documents that the large language models judged at that distance. For those pairs we report the percentage judged the same topic or the same specific content, and the percentage judged the same specific content. When most judged pairs at $r_{80}(n)$ are on the same topic, a corpus document within that distance of a sampled document is usually on the same topic as the sampled document.
