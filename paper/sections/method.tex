\section{Method}

\subsection{Setting and estimand}

A corpus is a finite set of $N$ documents $x_1, \dots, x_N$. Each is embedded by a fixed encoder and normalised, and the distance between two documents is the cosine distance of their embeddings. A sample $S$ is a set of $n$ documents drawn without replacement. The user chooses a resolution $r$: two documents within $r$ of each other count as the same content for the purpose at hand. Contextual embeddings fill a narrow cone of the space \citep{ethayarajh2019}, so the same cosine distance means different things for different encoders and corpora, and Section~\ref{sec:rcal} calibrates $r$ against judgments of content. The quantity to estimate, for the sample actually drawn, is the missing mass at resolution $r$,
\begin{equation}
M(S, r) = \frac{1}{N}\,\big|\{j : d(x_j, x_i) > r \text{ for all } i \in S\}\big| ,
\end{equation}
the share of corpus documents with no sampled document within $r$. Coverage is $1 - M$. The estimator is Maurer's $G$ of Equation~\ref{eq:G}, computed on the sample alone from each sampled document's distance to its nearest sampled neighbour. We write $G(n, r)$ for its value on a sample of size $n$.

The estimand is the finite corpus in hand. Sampling exists because the corpus is too large to annotate or to place in a model's context, and the full corpus is available to check the estimate.

\subsection{The collapse law}

Suppose the embedded documents lie near a set of intrinsic dimension $d$. The mass of a ball of radius $r$ around document $x$ is then about $c(x)\, r^{d}$, where $c(x)$ is proportional to the local density, and the probability that none of $n$ sampled documents falls in the ball is about $\exp(-c(x)\, n\, r^{d})$. Averaging over documents gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\, G(n, r) \approx \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\exp(-c(x)\, n\, r^{d})\big] = \mathcal{L}_{c}(n\, r^{d}),
\end{equation}
the Laplace transform of the distribution of $c$ evaluated at $n r^{d}$. Whatever the density, $G$ depends on $n$ and $r$ through the product $n r^{d}$ alone, so long as one $d$ holds across the radii of interest. The shape of the curve depends on how $c$ varies: a constant $c$ gives a pure exponential, and a spread of $c$ gives a curve that falls more slowly. We describe the shape by a stretched exponential, $\exp(-(\kappa\, n\, r^{d})^{b})$ with $b \le 1$, which is the Laplace transform of a particular mixing distribution, and fit
\begin{equation}
\log\big(-\log G(n, r)\big) = a + b \log\big(n\,r^{d}\big),
\label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
with $d$ estimated beforehand and $a$ and $b$ fitted by least squares. The grid has eleven sample sizes from 100 to 7{,}000 (capped at $0.7N$), twelve radii spaced geometrically from the 5th to the 99th percentile of a pilot's nearest neighbour distances, and five independent draws per sample size. Only cells with $0.02 < G < 0.98$ enter the fit. The law fails when the dimension itself changes with the radius, because no single $d$ then aligns the curves for different $r$.

The dimension $d$ comes from the TwoNN estimator of \citet{facco2017twonn}, which uses the ratio of each point's second to first nearest neighbour distance, averaged over five pilots of 2{,}000 documents. As a check that owes nothing to TwoNN, we also regress $\log(-\log G)$ on $\log n$ and $\log r$ separately. If the law holds, the coefficient on $\log r$ is $b\,d$ and the coefficient on $\log n$ is $b$, so their ratio estimates $d$.

Solving Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} for $n$ gives the sample size at which the isolation rate at resolution $r$ reaches a target $\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
n^{*}(\varepsilon, r) = \left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{e^{a}}\right)^{1/b} r^{-d} .
\label{eq:nstar}
\end{equation}
The corpus enters through three numbers. The dimension $d$ sets how fast the required sample grows as the resolution becomes finer: each halving of $r$ multiplies $n^{*}$ by $2^{d}$. The constants $a$ and $b$ set the level and are fitted on the pilot. 

\subsection{Finite population correction}

$G$ estimates the missing mass of the distribution that generated the corpus. When the estimand is the corpus itself, the $n$ sampled documents have distance zero to the sample, and each of the $N - n$ unsampled documents is isolated from the sample with probability close to $G$. The expected missing mass of the corpus is therefore
\begin{equation}
\hat M(n, r) = G(n, r)\,\big(1 - n/N\big).
\label{eq:fpc}
\end{equation}
The factor is the one survey sampling calls the finite population correction \citep{groves2009survey}. There it scales the variance of a mean, and here it scales an expectation, because sampled units are covered by definition.

\subsection{A diagnostic for corpora with more than one scale}

The law assumes one dimension across the radii of interest, and a user must know before sampling whether it holds. The diagnostic uses the pilot alone. Within one pilot of 2{,}000 documents, we fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of size up to 750 and compute $R^2$ on subsamples of size 1{,}000 to 2{,}000. A corpus passes when this pilot $R^2$ is at least 0.95. A second check needs no threshold: the ratio of coefficients from the two-regressor fit should agree with the TwoNN estimate of $d$. To validate the diagnostic we also compute the full out of sample $R^2$ on the whole corpus, from a fit on $n \le 1{,}000$ scored on $n$ up to 7{,}000, and call a corpus collapsing when it is at least 0.9. When the law fails, two probes locate the cause: the share of documents with a near duplicate (a nearest neighbour within cosine distance 0.02), and the spread of the TwoNN dimension across twenty $k$-means clusters.
