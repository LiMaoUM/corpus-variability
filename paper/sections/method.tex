\section{Method}

\subsection{Setting and estimand}

A corpus is a finite set of $N$ documents $x_1, \dots, x_N$. Each is embedded by a fixed encoder and normalised, and the distance between two documents is the cosine distance of their embeddings. A sample $S$ is a set of $n$ documents drawn without replacement. The user chooses a resolution $r$: two documents within $r$ of each other count as the same content for the purpose at hand. The quantity to estimate, for the sample actually drawn, is the missing mass at resolution $r$,
\begin{equation}
M(S, r) = \frac{1}{N}\,\big|\{j : d(x_j, x_i) > r \text{ for all } i \in S\}\big| ,
\end{equation}
the share of corpus documents with no sampled document within $r$. Coverage is $1 - M$. The estimator is Maurer's $G$ of Equation~\ref{eq:G}, computed on the sample alone from each sampled document's distance to its nearest sampled neighbour. We write $G(n, r)$ for its value on a sample of size $n$.

The estimand is the finite corpus in hand. Sampling exists because the corpus is too large to annotate or to place in a model's context, and the full corpus is available to check the estimate. This is the setting of the experiments below, and it is what makes a finite population correction necessary.

\subsection{The collapse law}

Suppose the embedded documents lie near a manifold of intrinsic dimension $d$ with a density that varies smoothly. The mass of a ball of radius $r$ around a document is then proportional to $r^{d}$, with a constant that depends on the local density, and the probability that none of $n$ sampled documents falls in the ball is about $\exp(-c\,n\,r^{d})$ for that constant $c$. Averaged over documents, the isolation rate $G$ should then depend on $n$ and $r$ only through the product $n r^{d}$. A density that varies across the manifold turns the single exponential into an average of exponentials with different rates. That average falls more slowly than any one exponential, and a stretched exponential, $\exp(-(c\,n\,r^{d})^{b})$ with $b < 1$, describes it well. We therefore fit
\begin{equation}
\log\big(-\log G(n, r)\big) = a + b \log\big(n\,r^{d}\big),
\label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
with $d$ estimated beforehand and $a$ and $b$ fitted by least squares. The cells are an $(n, r)$ grid of eleven sample sizes from 100 to 7{,}000 and twelve radii spaced geometrically from the 5th to the 99th percentile of a pilot's nearest neighbour distances, with one nested sample per $n$, and only cells with $0.02 < G < 0.98$ enter the fit. A value $b = 1$ is the pure exponential and $b < 1$ is the stretched form.

The dimension $d$ comes from the TwoNN estimator of \citet{facco2017twonn}, which uses only the ratio of each point's second to first nearest neighbour distance, on a pilot of 2{,}000 documents. As a check that owes nothing to TwoNN, we also regress $\log(-\log G)$ on $\log n$ and $\log r$ separately. If the law holds, the coefficient on $\log r$ is $b\,d$ and the coefficient on $\log n$ is $b$, so their ratio estimates $d$.

Solving Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} for $n$ gives the sample size at which the isolation rate at resolution $r$ reaches a target $\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
n^{*}(\varepsilon, r) = \left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{e^{a}}\right)^{1/b} r^{-d} .
\label{eq:nstar}
\end{equation}
This is the corpus analogue of $n = z^{2}\sigma^{2}/e^{2}$. The target $\varepsilon$ plays the part of the tolerated error, the resolution $r$ is set by the question, and $d$ is the property of the corpus that the formula needs, in the role of the variance. Each halving of $r$ multiplies $n^{*}$ by $2^{d}$.

\subsection{Finite population correction}

$G$ estimates the missing mass of the distribution that generated the corpus. When the estimand is the corpus itself, the $n$ sampled documents are covered at distance zero, and each of the $N - n$ unsampled documents is isolated from the sample with probability close to $G$. The corrected estimator is therefore
\begin{equation}
\hat M(n, r) = G(n, r)\,\big(1 - n/N\big),
\label{eq:fpc}
\end{equation}
with $1 - n/N$ the finite population correction of survey sampling. The correction is negligible when $n$ is a small share of $N$ and halves the estimate at $n = N/2$.

\subsection{A diagnostic for corpora with more than one scale}

Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} assumes one dimension for the whole corpus. The $R^2$ of the fit, and the $R^2$ on cells with $n > 1{,}000$ when the fit uses only cells with $n \le 1{,}000$, say whether the assumption holds. When it fails, two probes locate the cause: the share of documents with a near duplicate (a nearest neighbour within cosine distance 0.02), and the spread of the TwoNN dimension across twenty $k$-means clusters of the corpus.

\subsection{Theme coverage as a question about a partition}

A different question is how many documents are needed to see every theme. Once themes are a partition of the corpus with masses $m_1, \dots, m_K$, the expected mass of themes present in a sample of $n$ draws with replacement is
\begin{equation}
C_K(n) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} m_k\,\big(1 - (1 - m_k)^{n}\big),
\label{eq:gt}
\end{equation}
the Good-Turing expectation, and it depends on the corpus only through the masses. Our samples are drawn without replacement, and at $n \le 400$ from corpora of at least 7{,}600 documents the difference is below 0.003. We test the equation with inventories of $K = 30$ themes per corpus, against the exact label coverage of each sample and against the themes a language model reports from the sample. Section~\ref{sec:themes} shows that this curve and the missing mass at a fixed $r$ behave differently across corpora, which is why the two questions need two tools.
