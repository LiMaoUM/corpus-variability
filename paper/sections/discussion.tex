\section{Discussion}

The question we started from, whether 15 documents are enough to understand a corpus, has no single answer, and the reason is the main lesson of this paper. How much a sample covers depends on two things the reader rarely states. The first is what counts as the same content, which we made explicit as the resolution $r$. The second is a property of the corpus, its intrinsic dimension $d$, which sets how fast coverage grows with the sample. A corpus of tweets that repeat a few claims and a corpus of personal stories can both hold 10{,}000 documents, yet a reader of 15 of the tweets has seen most of their topics, while a reader of 1{,}000 of the stories has still seen little of the stories. Sample sizes copied from one corpus to another, such as the twelve interviews of qualitative research or the few hundred documents a large language model can read in one prompt, carry no guarantee.

For a researcher who samples text, the results suggest a short procedure. First, decide what the sample must cover, and give the resolution a meaning by having a few hundred pairs of pilot documents judged, by people or by a large language model, at a range of distances (Section~\ref{sec:rcal}). Second, embed a pilot of about 2{,}000 documents, estimate $d$ with TwoNN, and fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of the pilot. Third, check the fit with the pilot test. If the pilot $R^2$ is at least 0.95 and the two estimates of $d$ agree, take the sample size from Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}, and expect the true need to lie between two thirds and one and a half times that number. If the test fails, the corpus has more than one scale, and the fit should be trusted only for sample sizes inside the range the pilot covers. Fourth, after the sample is drawn, report $G\,(1 - n/N)$ as the share of the corpus that has nothing like it in the sample. This last step needs no pilot at all, and it already works on 15 documents.

The comparison with survey sampling holds in three places, each to a different degree. The factor $1 - n/N$ is the same finite population correction, applied to an expected share instead of a variance. The intrinsic dimension $d$ plays part of the role of the population variance. Like the variance, it is a property of the population, it is estimated from a pilot, and it sets how fast the required sample grows as the target becomes stricter. The margin of error is the third place, and here the two settings differ. A survey's margin of error bounds the error of an estimated proportion. For proportions, a corpus sample already does well: the plain share in a random sample was within 0.3 to 7 percentage points of the corpus share for the clusters we tested (Appendix~\ref{app:bound}). What a random sample cannot promise is that it contains an example of each kind of content. The missing mass measures exactly this risk, which matters most when the task is to describe or list what a corpus contains, as a summary or a qualitative codebook does. A report of a corpus sample can carry both numbers, the margin of error for proportions and the missing mass for content.

Coverage can also be measured against a list of themes, and this gives a different quantity. When themes divide the corpus into parts, the expected share of the theme mass that a sample contains depends only on how large the themes are, through $\sum_k m_k (1 - (1 - m_k)^{n})$ with $m_k$ the share of theme $k$. This expression matched 1{,}200 samples from eight corpora to within 0.4 percentage points on average (Appendix~\ref{app:themes}). Theme coverage is therefore set by the list of themes the researcher writes down: a coarse list is covered quickly and a fine list slowly, whatever the corpus. The isolation rate $G$ measures coverage at a fixed distance, independent of any list, and this is why it can tell a repetitive corpus from a diverse one.

Two extensions would make the method more useful. The first is a version for samples of accounts, since social media research often samples users and reads all of their posts. The second is a study of how the resolution that people would choose relates to the resolution chosen by large language models, which would replace our two automatic judges with a human standard.
