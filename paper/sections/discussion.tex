\section{Discussion}

The procedure a user follows has five steps, and Section~\ref{sec:feasible} shows its main use: learning from a pilot whether a coverage target is affordable, before the corpus is embedded. Choose the resolution $r$ that matches the question, by judging a few hundred pilot pairs binned by distance (Section~\ref{sec:rcal}). Embed a pilot of about 2{,}000 documents, estimate $d$ by TwoNN, and fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of the pilot. If the pilot $R^2$ is at least 0.95 and the two dimension estimates agree, take $n^{*}$ from Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} and allow a factor of 1.5 either way. If not, the corpus has more than one scale, the fit holds only for samples inside the range the pilot covers, and the probes of Section~5.4 say where the scales change. After the sample is drawn, report $G\,(1 - n/N)$ as the share of the corpus the sample leaves uncovered.

The survey analogy holds in one place and is instructive in two. The factor $1 - n/N$ is the survey correction, applied to an expectation. The dimension $d$ plays part of the role of the variance: it sets how fast the required sample grows as the target becomes finer, and it is estimated from a pilot. The margin of error is the third place. A corpus sample's plain mean already bounds the error of a proportion, to within 0.003 to 0.07 for cluster proportions (Appendix~\ref{app:bound}), while the missing mass bounds the content the sample has no example of, the error that matters when the task is to list what is in the corpus.

Coverage of a list of themes is a different quantity. When themes partition the corpus, the expected share of theme mass in a sample is set by the masses alone, and it matched 1{,}200 samples from eight corpora to a mean error of 0.004 (Appendix~\ref{app:themes}). It measures coverage relative to the inventory's granularity, while $G$ measures it at an absolute scale.
