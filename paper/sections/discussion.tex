\section{Discussion}

Whether 15 documents are enough to understand a corpus depends on the corpus and on the level of content the reader needs. On a repetitive corpus, 15 documents show nearly every topic, because the corpus returns again and again to a limited number of claims. At the level of specific content, 15 random documents covered no corpus we studied. A random sample of 15 can therefore support a list of the topics of a repetitive corpus, and it cannot support a statement about the claims, questions or stories of any corpus we studied.

The practical lesson concerns how a reading budget is spent. Where a researcher can embed the whole corpus, a chosen sample covers more than a random sample of the same size. We recommend dividing the embedded corpus with $k$-means into as many groups of similar documents as the budget allows, then reading the document nearest the center of each group. On a corpus that repeats itself, this choice covers far more of the corpus, because a single document read covers every document in its group that shares its content. On a corpus whose documents rarely share content, the chosen sample covers about as much as a random one, so the researcher loses nothing by choosing. $k$-means obtains most of what the near-best greedy design obtains, so a researcher can stop at a standard tool and does not need to search for the best possible set of documents.

A researcher who samples text can follow five steps. First, give the resolution $r$ a meaning in content by having large language models judge pairs of documents at a range of distances between their embeddings (Section~\ref{sec:rcal}), and take $r_{\mathrm{same}}$ or $r_{\mathrm{topic}}$ according to the level the study needs. Second, draw a random pilot of 100 documents and compute the isolation rate $G$ at that $r$. A $G$ of 0.99 or more means that almost no sampled document has another sampled document within $r$, so the corpus is diverse and choosing will save little. A $G$ of 0.7 or less means that the corpus repeats itself, so choosing will remove much of the missing mass. Third, choose the sample with $k$-means on the embedded corpus, or within a random pool of 5{,}000 embedded documents when the whole corpus cannot be embedded. When no document can be embedded, read a random sample and estimate how much of the corpus it missed with $G\,(1 - n/N)$. Fourth, when the study needs a sample size for a target, fit the law of Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on a pilot of 2{,}000 documents and apply the pilot test of Section~\ref{sec:diag}. The formula of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} is used only when the corpus passes that test. Fifth, report the missing mass with the sample. For a chosen sample, the missing mass is computed exactly from the embedded corpus. For a random sample, when the whole corpus has not been embedded, $G\,(1 - n/N)$ estimates the missing mass from the sample alone.

The measure follows survey sampling at four points. The first is the finite population correction. A sampled document covers itself, so the estimate multiplies $G$ by $1 - n/N$, the factor survey sampling uses for a sample drawn from a finite population \citep{cochran1977}. The second is the intrinsic dimension $d$, which plays the role of the variance. A survey needs more respondents when its population varies more, and a corpus needs more documents when its documents vary along more independent directions of meaning. The third is the design. Reading one document from each group of similar documents is the corpus counterpart of stratification \citep{kish1965}, which draws from every part of a population so that no part is left out. The fourth is the margin of error. For the percentage of a corpus that falls into a group of documents, a random sample works as a survey does, and Appendix~\ref{app:bound} shows that the percentage in the sample estimates the percentage in the corpus within 0.3 to 7 percentage points. Content has no such margin of error, because a corpus document counts as seen only when a document within $r$ of it was read, and the missing mass measures how much of the corpus remains unseen.

A researcher who already has a list of themes can ask a simpler question. When a researcher has a list of themes and knows the size of each in the corpus, the expected percentage of the corpus whose theme appears in a random sample depends only on those sizes. Appendix~\ref{app:themes} shows that this expectation matches the samples drawn from our corpora. The missing mass at $r$ needs no list of themes. It therefore applies before any theme has been named, which is the position of a researcher who samples in order to find the themes.

Three groups of researchers can use the measure now. Researchers who code text or discover topics with a large language model need a rule for when to stop. TopicGPT \citep{pham2024topicgpt} stops when 200 further documents add no new topic, and the missing mass offers a stopping rule stated as the percentage of the corpus not yet covered, estimated from the documents processed so far. \citet{edge2024graphrag} answer global questions over a whole corpus with query-focused summarization (Graph RAG). The missing mass of the documents behind such a summary tells how much of the corpus the summary can represent. \citet{lebronnec2024} measure the diversity of large language model output with precision and recall. With a set of generated texts as the sample and a reference set of human texts as the corpus, the missing mass measures how much of the human texts the generated texts cover. Two extensions follow from this study. Many studies of social media sample accounts and then read their posts, and the measure needs a version for such samples of accounts. The distances $r_{\mathrm{same}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{topic}}$ rest on judgments by large language models, and a human standard for $r$ would show how closely those judgments follow people.
