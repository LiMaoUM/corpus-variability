\section{Discussion}

The question we started from, whether 15 documents are enough to understand a corpus, has no single answer. How much a sample covers depends on two things the reader rarely states. The first is what counts as the same content, which we made explicit as the resolution $r$. The second is a property of the corpus, its intrinsic dimension $d$, which sets how fast coverage grows with the sample. A corpus of tweets that repeat a few claims and a corpus of personal stories can both hold 10{,}000 documents, yet a reader of 15 of the tweets has seen most of their topics, while a reader of 1{,}000 of the stories has still seen little of the stories. A sample size that worked in one setting, such as the dozen interviews after which qualitative researchers often stop, or the few hundred documents a large language model can take in one prompt, therefore carries no guarantee for another corpus.

For a researcher who samples text, the results suggest a short procedure. First, decide what the sample must cover, and give the resolution a meaning by having a few hundred pairs of pilot documents judged, by people or by a large language model, at a range of distances (Section~\ref{sec:rcal}). Second, embed a pilot of about 2{,}000 documents, estimate $d$ with TwoNN, and fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of the pilot. Third, check the fit with the pilot test. If the pilot $R^2$ is at least 0.95 and the two estimates of $d$ agree, take the sample size from Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}, and expect the true need to lie between two thirds and one and a half times that number. If the test fails, the corpus has more than one scale, and the fit should be trusted only for sample sizes inside the range the pilot covers. Fourth, after the sample is drawn, report $G\,(1 - n/N)$ as the share of the corpus that has nothing like it in the sample. Once $r$ is set, this last step needs no pilot fit, and it already works on 15 documents.

The comparison with survey sampling can be made at three points. The factor $1 - n/N$ is the same finite population correction, applied to an expected share instead of a variance. The intrinsic dimension $d$ plays part of the role of the population variance. Like the variance, it is a property of the population, it is estimated from a pilot, and it sets how fast the required sample grows as the target becomes stricter. The margin of error is the third place, and here the two settings differ. A survey's margin of error bounds the error of an estimated proportion. For proportions, the plain share in a random sample already estimates the corpus share, within 0.3 to 7 percentage points for the shares of the fifty clusters we tested (Appendix~\ref{app:bound}). A random sample does not, however, promise to contain an example of each kind of content. The missing mass measures this risk, which matters most when the task is to describe or list what a corpus contains, as a summary or a qualitative codebook does. A report of a corpus sample can carry both numbers, the margin of error for proportions and the missing mass for content.

Coverage can also be measured against a list of themes, and this gives a different quantity. When themes divide the corpus into parts, the expected share of the theme mass that a sample contains depends only on how large the themes are, through $\sum_k m_k (1 - (1 - m_k)^{n})$ with $m_k$ the share of theme $k$. This expression matched 1{,}200 samples from eight corpora to within 0.4 percentage points on average (Appendix~\ref{app:themes}). Theme coverage therefore depends on the sizes of the themes in the list the researcher writes down. A list of a few large themes is covered quickly, and a list of many small themes slowly. The isolation rate $G$ measures coverage at a fixed distance, without any list, so two corpora with lists of the same size can still differ in $G$, as a repetitive corpus and a diverse one do.

Two extensions would make the method more useful. The first is a version for samples of accounts, since social media research often samples users and reads all of their posts. The second is a study of how the resolution that people would choose relates to the resolution chosen by large language models, which would replace our two automatic judges with a human standard.
