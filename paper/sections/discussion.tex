\section{Discussion}

The procedure a user follows has five steps, and Section~\ref{sec:feasible} shows its main use: learning from a pilot whether a coverage target is affordable, before the corpus is embedded. Choose the resolution $r$ that matches the question, by judging a few hundred pilot pairs binned by distance (Section~\ref{sec:rcal}). Embed a pilot of about 2{,}000 documents, estimate $d$ by TwoNN, and fit Equation~\ref{eq:collapse} on subsamples of the pilot. If the pilot $R^2$ is at least 0.95 and the two dimension estimates agree, take $n^{*}$ from Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} and allow a factor of 1.5 either way. If not, the corpus has more than one scale, and the probes of Section~5.4 say where. After the sample is drawn, report $G\,(1 - n/N)$ as the share of the corpus the sample leaves uncovered. Only the first step needs judgments.

The survey analogy holds in one place and is instructive in two. The factor $1 - n/N$ is the same as the survey correction, applied here to an expectation. The dimension $d$ plays part of the role of the variance: it is the corpus property that sets how fast the required sample grows as the target becomes finer, and like the variance it is estimated from a pilot. The margin of error is the third place. A survey's margin bounds the error of a proportion, and a corpus sample's plain mean already does that job, to within 0.003 to 0.07 for cluster proportions (Appendix~\ref{app:bound}). The missing mass bounds the mass of content the sample has no example of, which is the error that matters when the task is to list what is in the corpus. A report of a corpus sample can carry both numbers.

Coverage of a list of themes is a different quantity. When themes partition the corpus with masses $m_1, \dots, m_K$, the expected share of theme mass in a sample of $n$ is $\sum_k m_k (1 - (1 - m_k)^{n})$, set by the masses alone, and it matched 1{,}200 samples from eight corpora to a mean error of 0.004 (Appendix~\ref{app:themes}). A theme inventory thus measures coverage relative to its own granularity. Breadth at an absolute scale is visible only to a measure at that scale, which is what $G$ provides.
