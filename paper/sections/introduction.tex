\section{Introduction}

Large collections of text are sampled before they are studied. An annotation team codes a few thousand posts out of millions, a language model summarizes a few hundred documents because the corpus does not fit its context, and a topic model is fitted to a slice because the full run is slow. The sampler of a human population has a settled answer to the question such a slice raises. Given the population variance and a target error, the formula $n = z^{2}\sigma^{2}/e^{2}$ gives the sample size, and the estimate comes with a margin of error \citep{cochran1977, kish1965}. The sampler of a corpus has no such formula. The sample size is set by budget, and whether the slice covers the corpus is left unasked.

The question has an exact form once a corpus is a set of points. Embed each document, choose a distance $r$ that says what counts as the same content, and ask what share of the corpus lies farther than $r$ from every sampled document. That share is the missing mass at resolution $r$. In the discrete case, where two documents either share a category or do not, it is the quantity Good and Turing estimated from the number of categories seen once \citep{good1953}, and ecologists have built a sampling theory on it, with rarefaction and extrapolation curves that say how many more units a target coverage needs \citep{chao2012coverage}. \citet{maurer2022missing} extended the estimator to metric spaces. His $G$ is the share of sampled points with no other sampled point within $r$, and he proved its bias and variance bounds. The estimator has had no application to text, and nothing in it says how $G$ falls as the sample grows.

This paper turns $G$ into a sample size formula for corpora and tests it on ten corpora. Four results carry the paper.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The isolation rate $G(n, r)$ depends on $n$ and $r$ only through $n r^{d}$, where $d$ is the intrinsic dimension of the embedded corpus. A single curve fitted on samples of at most 1{,}000 documents predicts the isolation rate at 7{,}000 documents with a mean error of 0.03 to 0.05 on AskDocs, TIFU and a tweet corpus, and the fit recovers $d$ on synthetic manifolds of known dimension. The required sample size for a target isolation rate at resolution $r$ then follows in closed form, and $d$, estimated from a pilot of 2{,}000 documents, is the corpus property that sets it.
\item For a finite corpus of $N$ documents the missing mass is $G\,(1 - n/N)$. The correction is the one survey sampling applies to a finite population, and it brings the error of $G$ against the true missing mass from 0.07 to 0.01.
\item The collapse fails on one corpus of political posts whose clusters have local dimension from 2 to 16, slogans beside discussion, and it keeps failing after near duplicates are removed. The $R^2$ of the collapse fit is therefore a diagnostic that says whether one dimension describes a corpus.
\item How many documents are needed to see every theme is a different question. For a fixed number of themes its answer depends on the theme masses alone, and it is the same curve in all eight corpora we tested, 0.82 to 0.86 of the theme mass at $n = 50$. Coverage at a fixed resolution differs tenfold across the same corpora. The two questions need two tools, and we say which answers which.
\end{enumerate}

In practice the procedure is short. A user picks the resolution $r$ that matches the question, embeds a pilot, estimates $d$, and takes the sample size and the coverage band. If the collapse fit is poor the corpus has more than one scale, and the user is told so before the sample is drawn.
