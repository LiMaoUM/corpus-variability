\section{Introduction}

Large collections of text are sampled before they are studied. An annotation team codes a few thousand posts out of millions, a language model summarizes a few hundred documents because the corpus does not fit its context, and a topic model is fitted to a slice because the full run is slow. The sampler of a human population has a settled answer to the question such a slice raises. Given the population variance $\sigma^{2}$ and a tolerated error $e$, the formula $n = z^{2}\sigma^{2}/e^{2}$ gives the sample size, and the estimate comes with a margin of error \citep{cochran1977, kish1965}. The sampler of a corpus has no such formula. The sample size is set by budget, and whether the slice covers the corpus is left unasked.

The question has an exact form once a corpus is a set of points. Embed each document, choose a distance $r$ that says what counts as the same content, and ask what share of the corpus lies farther than $r$ from every sampled document. We call that share the missing mass at resolution $r$. In the discrete case, where two documents either share a category or do not, it is the quantity Good and Turing estimated from the share of draws whose category was seen once \citep{good1953}. Ecologists built a sampling theory on that estimate: a rarefaction curve gives the coverage a smaller sample would have had, an extrapolation curve gives the coverage a larger sample will have, and together they say how many more units a target coverage needs \citep{chao2012coverage}. \citet{maurer2022missing} carried the estimator into metric spaces. His statistic $G$ is the share of sampled points with no other sampled point within $r$, and he proved bounds on its bias and variance. The estimator has had no application to text, and nothing in Maurer's treatment says how $G$ falls as the sample grows.

This paper turns $G$ into a sample size formula for corpora and tests the formula on ten real corpora. Four results carry the paper.

\begin{enumerate}
\item $G(n, r)$ depends on $n$ and $r$ only through $n r^{d}$, where $d$ is the intrinsic dimension of the embedded corpus. A curve fitted on samples of at most 1{,}000 documents predicts $G$ at 7{,}000 documents to a mean error of 0.02 to 0.05 on five real corpora, and the fit recovers $d$ on synthetic manifolds of known dimension. The sample size for a target isolation rate at resolution $r$ then follows in closed form, and it lands within a factor of 1.5 of the empirical sample size in 20 of 24 tested cases on corpora that obey the law. The corpus property the formula needs is $d$, estimated from a pilot of 2{,}000 documents.
\item For a finite corpus of $N$ documents the missing mass is $G\,(1 - n/N)$. The factor is the finite population correction of survey sampling, and it brings the mean error of $G$ against the true missing mass from 0.07 to 0.015.
\item On one corpus of political posts the law fails. Its clusters have local dimension from 2 to 16, slogans beside discussion, and the failure persists after near duplicates are removed. The $R^2$ of the fit is therefore a diagnostic that says whether one dimension describes a corpus.
\item How many documents are needed to see every theme is a different question. Once themes are a partition of the corpus, the answer depends on the theme masses alone. With thirty themes per corpus it is the same curve in all eight corpora we tested, 0.82 to 0.86 of the theme mass at $n = 50$, while coverage at a fixed resolution runs from 0.003 to 0.77 across the same corpora at $n = 25$. The two questions need two tools, and we say which answers which.
\end{enumerate}

In practice the procedure is short. A user picks the resolution $r$ that matches the question, embeds a pilot, estimates $d$, and takes the sample size from the formula. After the sample is drawn, $G\,(1 - n/N)$ reports the share of the corpus it leaves uncovered. If the fit is poor the corpus has more than one scale, and the user is told so before the sample is drawn.
