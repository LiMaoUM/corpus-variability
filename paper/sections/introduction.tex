\section{Introduction}

Researchers who study large collections of text work with samples, because the collections contain more text than people or models can process. An annotation team labels as many posts as its budget pays for. A large language model takes in only as much text as fits in its context window, the amount of text it can process at once. Qualitative researchers stop interviewing at saturation, when new interviews stop adding new themes, which the literature places at about 9 to 17 interviews \citep{guest2006,hennink2022}. TopicGPT, a method that finds the topics of a corpus with a large language model, stops when 200 further documents add no new topic \citep{pham2024topicgpt}. In each case a budget or a convention sets the size of the sample. If we read 15 posts from a corpus of 10{,}000, can we be sure we basically understand the corpus? Would 100 or 1{,}000 posts be enough?

Survey research answers the same question for an estimate about a population. Before the survey, a sample size formula tells the designer how many respondents a target margin of error requires \citep{cochran1977,kish1965}. After the survey, the margin of error tells the reader how far the estimate may lie from the population value. A sample of a corpus comes with neither. The question a reader of such a sample has in mind is how much of the corpus has a similar document among the documents read. This paper builds an estimate of that percentage and a sample size formula for it.

The question needs a rule for when a sampled document represents a document of the corpus. A text encoder turns each document into an embedding, a list of numbers, so that documents with similar meaning have embeddings close together. We measure the distance between two documents as the cosine distance between their embeddings, which is 0 for the same meaning and near 1 for unrelated documents. Two documents count as the same content when the distance between them is smaller than a resolution $r$ chosen by the researcher. A researcher who needs the same specific claim chooses a small $r$, and one who needs only the same topic chooses a larger one. To give $r$ a meaning in content, two large language models judged pairs of documents at a range of distances. Each pair was judged as the same specific content (the same claim, question, event or story), the same topic, or unrelated. The judgments tell which distance means the same specific content and which means the same topic.

With $r$ fixed, a corpus document is missed when no sampled document lies within $r$ of it. The missing mass is the percentage of the corpus that is missed, and the rest of the corpus is covered. A researcher who holds only the sample cannot count the missed documents directly. Good and Turing faced the same problem with words, and estimated the probability that the next word of a text is one not yet seen from the words seen exactly once \citep{good1953}. \citet{maurer2022missing} extended this estimator from words to distances. Its input is what we call the isolation rate $G$, the percentage of sampled documents with no other sampled document within $r$. A sample of $n$ documents from a corpus of $N$, such as 15 from 10{,}000, estimates the missing mass as $G\,(1 - n/N)$. The factor $1 - n/N$ is the finite population correction of survey sampling, and it is needed because each sampled document covers itself. Maurer develops the estimator in theory, and in a search of the literature in 2026 we found no work that applies it to text.

Annotation and summarization usually draw the documents they read at random, yet work that picks data for a model to learn from chooses them. A text encoder embeds a corpus without any human reading or any call to a large language model, so this work can choose with every document in view. It divides the embedded documents with $k$-means \citep{lloyd1982} into as many groups of similar documents as the budget allows. It then labels the document nearest the center of each group, the average embedding of the group's documents \citep{chang2021training, yuan2020coldstart, hacohen2022active, zhang2023autocot}. These studies judge the chosen documents by how well a model trained or prompted with them performs. None of them reports how much of the corpus the chosen documents cover. The design resembles stratified sampling in a survey, which divides a population into strata, groups such as regions, so that every part of the population appears in the sample. The two designs differ within each group. A stratified sample draws respondents at random within each stratum and weights each stratum by its size, so it still estimates how common each answer is in the population. The $k$-means design takes the one document nearest each center, whatever the size of the group. A chosen sample therefore serves coverage, and it does not estimate how common each topic or claim is in the corpus. Whether choosing helps depends on how much the corpus repeats itself. If many documents make the same claim, one document from each group represents many others. If documents rarely share specific content, a document unlike every other can be covered only by reading it, and no choice of documents can help a researcher who needs the same specific content.

Embedding needs only the documents in hand, so what a researcher can choose from depends on which documents the researcher holds. A researcher who holds the whole corpus embeds all of it and chooses from it by $k$-means. Because every document is embedded, the missing mass of the chosen sample is then computed exactly. Some researchers can obtain a large random sample of the corpus but not the whole corpus, because a platform's interface returns only a sample of its posts or because the corpus is too large to store. Such a researcher embeds a random pool of documents and chooses within it, and the pool alone shows how much the chosen sample misses. Other researchers can obtain only the documents they read, because each document costs money or requires access. They draw these documents at random and estimate the missing mass with $G\,(1 - n/N)$.

How fast the missing mass falls as the sample grows follows from a classical geometric result about nearest neighbors among randomly scattered points \citep{penrose2003rgg, levina2005}. The intrinsic dimension $d$ of a corpus is the number of independent ways in which its documents differ in meaning. We find that, on most of the corpora we tested, the isolation rate follows a law in which the sample size $n$ and the resolution $r$ enter only through the product $n r^{d/2}$, so that any two samples with the same product leave the same percentage of their documents isolated. A larger sample and a looser resolution therefore trade against each other at a fixed rate set by $d$. TwoNN estimates $d$ from the ratio of each document's distance to its closest other document and its distance to the second closest \citep{facco2017twonn}. Under the law, halving $r$ requires $2^{d/2}$ times as many documents, which for $d = 26$ is about 8{,}000 times as many. The dimension $d$ does for a corpus what the variance does for a survey population. A population with a larger variance needs more respondents for the same margin of error, and a corpus with a larger $d$ needs more documents for the same resolution.

We test the estimate, the choice of documents and the formula on synthetic data with a known answer and on ten real corpora: three Reddit forums, five slices of 2020 tweets about the United States Census, four of them selected by a keyword, and random samples of English posts from Truth Social and Bluesky in 2024. The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A measure of what a sample of text has missed, the missing mass at a resolution chosen by the researcher, which the sample alone estimates through the isolation rate and the finite population correction of survey sampling.
\item A measurement of how much of a corpus a sample chosen by $k$-means covers, against a random sample of the same size, at distances between documents' embeddings that large language models judged to mark the same topic or the same specific content. The chosen sample covers far more of the corpus on corpora that repeat themselves. The isolation rate of a random pilot of 100 documents tells in advance how much of the missing mass choosing will remove.
\item A sample size formula, obtained by solving the law for $n$, with a pilot test that tells whether the formula can be trusted on a given corpus. The pilot test fits the law on small subsamples of a pilot sample and checks it on larger subsamples of the same pilot.
\item A way to give the resolution $r$ a meaning in content, by having large language models judge pairs of documents.
\end{enumerate}
