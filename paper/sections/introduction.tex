\section{Introduction}

Large collections of text are sampled before they are studied. An annotation team codes a few thousand posts out of millions, a language model summarizes a few hundred documents because the corpus does not fit its context, and a topic model is fitted to a slice because the full run is slow. The sampler of a human population has a settled answer to the question such a slice raises. Given the population variance $\sigma^{2}$ and a tolerated error $e$, the formula $n = z^{2}\sigma^{2}/e^{2}$ gives the sample size, and the estimate comes with a margin of error \citep{cochran1977, kish1965}. The sampler of a corpus has no such formula. The sample size is set by budget, and whether the slice covers the corpus is left unasked.

The question has an exact form once a corpus is a set of points. Embed each document, choose a distance $r$ that says what counts as the same content, and ask what share of the corpus lies farther than $r$ from every sampled document. We call that share the missing mass at resolution $r$. In the discrete case, where two documents either share a category or do not, it is the quantity Good and Turing estimated from the share of draws whose category was seen once \citep{good1953}. Ecologists built a sampling theory on that estimate: a rarefaction curve gives the coverage a smaller sample would have had, an extrapolation curve gives the coverage a larger sample will have, and together they say how many more units a target coverage needs \citep{chao2012coverage}. \citet{maurer2022missing} carried the estimator into metric spaces. His statistic $G$ is the share of sampled points with no other sampled point within $r$, and he proved bounds on its bias and variance. The estimator has had no application to text, and Maurer's treatment says nothing about how $G$ falls as the sample grows.

How $G$ falls follows from a classical result. For points drawn from a density on a $d$-dimensional set, the chance that a point has no neighbour within $r$ among $n$ others decays like $\exp(-c\,n\,r^{d})$, the law behind nearest neighbour estimators of intrinsic dimension \citep{levina2005, facco2017twonn} and behind the isolated vertices of a random geometric graph \citep{penrose2003rgg}. We take this law, which has not been used to plan samples, and test whether it holds well enough on embedded text to set a sample size. Three results carry the paper.

\begin{enumerate}
\item On corpora where the law holds, $G$ depends on $n$ and $r$ through $n r^{d}$, with $d$ the intrinsic dimension estimated from a pilot of 2{,}000 documents and two shape constants fitted on the pilot. A fit on 1{,}000 documents predicts $G$ at seven times that size with an out of sample $R^2$ above 0.92. The sample size it prescribes for a target isolation rate falls within a factor of 1.5 of the empirical sample size in 77 of 83 cases, over ten such pairs of corpus and encoder. The Chao and Jost extrapolation, the standard ecological tool, errs two to nine times more within its own range.
\item For a finite corpus of $N$ documents the missing mass is $G\,(1 - n/N)$, because the sampled documents are covered by definition. The correction cuts the mean error tenfold, to 0.012.
\item The law fails on some corpora of political posts, where the effective dimension changes with the radius, slogans beside discussion. A fit inside the pilot flags every one of them before the sample is drawn, and on them the formula's predictions fall within a factor of 1.5 in only 10 of 23 cases.
\end{enumerate}

With the resolution calibrated to mean ``the same specific content'', a pilot separates two kinds of corpus. On corpora that repeat a few claims, a few hundred documents cover half of the content. On forum questions, stories and Truth Social threads, covering half of the corpus takes a sample of half of it, as it would if no two documents shared content, so a sample covers what it contains and an analysis that needs every claim must read every document.

In practice the procedure is short. A user embeds a pilot, picks the resolution $r$ that matches the question by judging a few hundred pairs, estimates $d$ and the two constants, checks the fit, and takes the sample size from the formula. After the sample is drawn, $G\,(1 - n/N)$ reports the share of the corpus it leaves uncovered.
