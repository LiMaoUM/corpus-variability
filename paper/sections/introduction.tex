\section{Introduction}

Researchers who study large collections of text can rarely read all of them, because the collections hold far more text than people or models can process. An annotation team codes a few thousand posts out of millions. A large language model summarizes a few hundred documents, because the whole corpus does not fit in its context window. Qualitative researchers stop collecting data at saturation, when new interviews stop adding new codes, which in empirical tests happens after about 9 to 17 interviews \citep{guest2006, hennink2022}. In each case the researcher works with a sample, and the size of the sample is usually set by the budget or by convention. If we read 15 posts from a corpus of 10{,}000, a number a person can read in a few minutes, have we understood the corpus? If not, would 100 posts be enough, or 1{,}000?

Survey research has a settled answer to the same question when it estimates a quantity for a human population. The researcher states how much people differ on the quantity of interest (the population variance $\sigma^{2}$) and how large an error is tolerable ($e$), and the formula, with $z$ the normal quantile for the chosen confidence level, $n = z^{2}\sigma^{2}/e^{2}$ gives the number of people from whom to collect data \citep{cochran1977, kish1965}. Every estimate from the sample then carries a margin of error. A sample of a corpus comes with neither a sample size formula nor a margin of error. This paper builds both for the question a reader of a sample usually has in mind, namely how much of the corpus has a document like it among the documents we read.

To answer this question, we first need to say when a document in the sample stands for a document in the corpus. Two posts can be about the same topic, such as the 2020 United States Census, while making different claims, and two other posts can repeat the same claim in different words. We place each document in an embedding space, where a text encoder puts documents with similar meaning close together, and we measure how alike two documents are by the cosine distance between them. The researcher then chooses a distance $r$, and two documents closer than $r$ count as the same content. A number such as $r = 0.2$ means little by itself, so we asked large language models to judge pairs of documents at a range of distances. This tells, for each corpus, which distance corresponds to the same specific claim, question, event or story, and which corresponds to the same general topic.

Once $r$ is fixed, the percentage of the corpus that has no document within $r$ in the sample is called the missing mass, and statistics has a classical way to estimate it without looking at the unread documents. During the Second World War, I.~J. Good and Alan Turing noticed that a sample reveals how much it is missing through the items it contains only once \citep{good1953}. If most words in a sample of text occur once, many more words remain unseen. If every word in the sample has occurred several times, few words remain. A word seen once is the kind of word a sample could easily have missed, so the percentage of such words estimates how much the sample has missed. \citet{maurer2022missing} carried this idea from words, which either match or do not, to items separated by a distance. His estimator $G$, which we call the isolation rate, is the percentage of sampled documents that have no other sampled document within $r$, that is, the documents that are the only one of their kind in the sample. Maurer proved that $G$ is close to the missing mass on average and bounded its error, and his paper contains no data. It leaves open how $G$ falls as the sample grows, which is what a researcher needs in order to plan a sample. To our knowledge the estimator has not yet been used on text.

How $G$ falls with the sample size follows from a classical result about the distances between random points \citep{levina2005, facco2017twonn, penrose2003rgg}. Suppose the documents of a corpus differ from one another along $d$ independent directions of meaning, where $d$ is called the intrinsic dimension of the embedded corpus and can be estimated from the distances between documents. Then a larger sample and a looser distance can stand in for each other at a fixed rate. When the definition of the same content becomes twice as strict, that is, when $r$ is halved, the region around a document within which another document counts as a match shrinks $2^{d}$ times. A sample must then be $2^{d}$ times as large to cover the corpus as well as before. If $d$ is 10, a definition twice as strict requires about 1{,}000 times as many documents. Formally, $G$ depends on $n$ and $r$ only through the product $n r^{d}$, so that any two samples with the same product leave the same percentage of their documents isolated. The intrinsic dimension thus does for a corpus what the variance does for a survey population. It is the property that sets how large a sample must be.

This paper turns this idea into a tool for sampling text and tests it on ten real corpora, among them medical questions, personal stories, election discussion, tweets about the 2020 Census, and posts from Truth Social and Bluesky, and on synthetic data with a known answer. It makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A measure of what a sample of text has missed, the missing mass at a distance $r$, with an estimate that needs only the sample. For a finite corpus the estimate needs one correction, because the sampled documents are covered by themselves. It is the same factor that survey sampling calls the finite population correction.
\item A sample size formula for text. It rests on a law that holds on most of the corpora we tested, namely the fixed rate at which sample size and distance trade against each other, and it tells a researcher how many documents bring the missing mass down to a target.
\item A test, run on a pilot of 2{,}000 documents, that shows before the full sample is drawn whether the formula can be trusted for a given corpus.
\item A way to give the distance $r$ a meaning in terms of content, by having large language models judge pairs of documents, so that the missing mass answers a question a reader can state: how much of the corpus has a document on the same specific content, or on the same topic, in the sample?
\end{enumerate}
