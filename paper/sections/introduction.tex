\section{Introduction}

Researchers who study large collections of text rarely read all of them. An annotation team codes a few thousand posts out of millions, a large language model summarizes a few hundred documents because the whole corpus does not fit in its context window, and a qualitative researcher keeps reading until new documents stop adding anything new. Each of these practices rests on the same assumption, that the documents in hand stand for the documents left unread. Yet the number of documents is usually set by budget or by feel. If we read 15 posts from a corpus, have we understood the corpus? If not, would 100 posts be enough, or 1{,}000?

Survey research has a settled answer to the same question for a human population. The researcher states how much people differ on the quantity of interest (the population variance $\sigma^{2}$) and how large an error is tolerable ($e$), and the formula $n = z^{2}\sigma^{2}/e^{2}$ gives the number of people to interview \citep{cochran1977, kish1965}. Every estimate from the sample then carries a margin of error. A sample of a corpus comes with neither a sample size formula nor a margin of error. In this paper we build both for one question, the question a reader of a sample usually has in mind: what share of the corpus has nothing like it among the documents we read?

The answer depends on what counts as ``like it''. Two posts can be about the same topic, such as the 2020 United States Census, while making different claims, and two other posts can repeat the same claim in different words. We place each document in an embedding space, where documents with similar meaning lie close together, and we let the user choose a distance $r$ below which two documents count as the same content. To give $r$ a concrete meaning, we asked two large language models to judge about 4{,}000 pairs of documents. For each corpus we then found the distance at which half of the pairs are about the same specific claim, question, event or story, and the distance at which half of the pairs share at least a topic (Section~\ref{sec:rcal}).

At these two distances the question of the 15 posts has a sharp answer, and the answer differs from corpus to corpus (Table~\ref{tab:reader}). Take the tweets about the 2020 Census that contain the word ``illegal''. Fifteen random tweets leave only 1.8\% of the corpus without a tweet on the same topic in the sample, so a reader of those 15 tweets has seen nearly every topic the corpus discusses. The same 15 tweets leave 62\% of the corpus without a tweet that makes the same specific point. Now take a corpus of personal stories from the TIFU forum on Reddit. Fifteen stories leave 98.5\% of the corpus without a story even on the same topic, and 1{,}000 stories still leave 90\% of the corpus without a story about the same event. Whether a sample is enough therefore depends on the corpus and on what the reader needs to know. The same number of documents can show nearly everything in one corpus and almost nothing in another.

The share of a corpus that has nothing like it in the sample is called the missing mass, and statistics has a classical way to estimate it without looking at the unread documents. During the Second World War, I.~J. Good and Alan Turing noticed that a sample reveals how much it is missing through the items it contains only once \citep{good1953}. If most words in a sample of text occur once, many more words remain unseen. If every word in the sample has occurred several times, few words remain. \citet{maurer2022missing} carried this idea from categories to distances. His estimator $G$, which we call the isolation rate, is the share of documents in the sample that have no other sampled document within $r$, that is, the share of the documents we read that were unlike anything else we read. In the Census tweets that contain ``illegal'', 64\% of 15 random tweets have no close match among the other 14, and the share of the corpus those 15 tweets leave unseen is 62\%. A reader can therefore learn how much a sample has missed from the sample alone. Maurer's paper is theoretical and uses no data. The estimator has not been applied to text, and Maurer says nothing about how $G$ falls as the sample grows, which is what a researcher needs to plan a sample.

How $G$ falls with the sample size follows from a classical result about the distances between random points \citep{levina2005, facco2017twonn, penrose2003rgg}. Suppose the documents of a corpus differ from one another along $d$ independent directions of meaning, where $d$ is called the intrinsic dimension of the embedded corpus. Then the chance that a document has no neighbour within $r$ among $n$ sampled documents depends on $n$ and $r$ only through the product $n r^{d}$. The product has a direct meaning for sampling. When the definition of the same content becomes twice as strict, that is, when $r$ is halved, each document has $2^{d}$ times fewer documents close enough to count as a match, so the sample must grow $2^{d}$ times to cover the corpus as well as before. For the AskDocs and TIFU corpora in this paper $d$ is about 13, so a definition twice as strict requires about 8{,}000 times as many documents. The intrinsic dimension thus plays the role that the variance plays in a survey: it is the property of the corpus that sets how large a sample must be.

We test whether this law holds well enough on real text to set a sample size, on seventeen pairs of corpus and encoder that include medical questions, personal stories, election discussion, tweets about the 2020 Census, and posts from Truth Social and Bluesky. We find three things.

\begin{enumerate}
\item On ten of the seventeen pairs, $G$ depends on the sample size and the distance only through $n r^{d}$. A fit on samples of up to 1{,}000 documents predicts $G$ for samples seven times larger, and the sample size the law prescribes is close to the size actually needed. In 77 of 83 tests, the sample that actually reached a coverage target held between two thirds of the formula's number and one and a half times that number, so a formula answer of 1{,}500 documents meant an actual need between 1{,}000 and 2{,}250. The standard tool from ecology for the same task, the extrapolation of \citet{chao2012coverage}, misses the coverage of a larger sample by two to nine times as much as the law does.
\item For a corpus of $N$ documents, the share left unseen is $G\,(1 - n/N)$. The correction is needed because the sampled documents themselves have been seen, and survey sampling knows it as the finite population correction. With it, the estimate is off by about one percentage point on average.
\item The law fails on some corpora of political posts, in which short slogans repeated many times sit beside longer discussion. A test run on a pilot sample of 2{,}000 documents flags every one of these corpora before the full sample is drawn.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, these results let a researcher answer the question of the 15 posts before reading. On corpora that repeat a few claims, such as Census tweets selected by a keyword or a forum about one election, a few hundred posts are enough for half of the corpus to have a post with the same content in the sample. On forum questions, personal stories and Truth Social threads, a sample covers what it contains and little else. For half of such a corpus to have a match in the sample, the sample must hold more than 40\% of the corpus, close to the 50\% it would need if no two documents in the corpus shared content. A pilot of 100 documents tells the two kinds of corpus apart, and a pilot of 2{,}000 documents predicts the required sample for the corpus at hand.
